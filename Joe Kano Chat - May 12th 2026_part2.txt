On reflection.
This is where the muddiness was. The question is not should he reflect — every consciousness reflects. The question is what does his reflection look like, and the answer the book has already implied is: he does not reflect well. His mind goes places and stops. He starts a thought about the past and the thought wanders into a drink order or a song on the bar speakers or a woman at the next table. On victimisation.
This one is the most interesting and the easiest to get wrong. Your instinct is correct: he cannot be a self-pitier as his stated mode. That is unreadable. Self-pity that leaks past a man's defences against it is human.
Joe does not say life was unfair to me. Joe does not say I was misunderstood. Joe says nothing of the kind. The same archetypes Joe is surrounded by exist in Havana, in Buzios, in Costa Rica, with different costumes. The man drinking two bottles of Havana Gold a day in a shithole rented from a señora with a portrait of the Virgin and Che on the same wall. The German running a hostel in Buzios who married a local and now does not leave. The American in Tamarindo who came on holiday seventeen years ago and never reactivated his passport. These are the same man as Joe. Different climate, different liquor, different church. The ecology is transferable because the ecology is what the global expat-bachelor circuit produces wherever it lands — wherever there is a price differential, a language barrier just thin enough, and women whose options the local economy has compressed.
This matters for the book in a concrete way: it gives you the right to write Manila with confidence not because you lived there longest but because you have triangulated the type. The writer who has lived in only one such place writes a book that risks being about that place. The writer who has lived in four writes a book about the thing, with one place as the specific setting. The Manila of Joe Cano benefits from the Havana of you and the Buzios of you because you can see what is Manila-specific versus what is just what these ecologies do everywhere. The Manila-specific things stay; the universal things you can use without worrying that you are over-claiming knowledge.
The cigar-rolling-in-the-guayabera-passing-as-local is also exactly the kind of biographical fact that is useful as a private check on the prose without ever appearing in it. When you write a scene of Joe in his cotton barong being addressed in Tagalog at a kiosk because the vendor cannot read him as foreign at a glance, you know what that experience is from inside. Not theoretically. Not from research. From having had vendors in Havana take you for a Camagüeyano. The prose carries the weight of the having-been-there even though Cuba is never named.
The hookers-not-propositioning-you detail is the strongest of the lot for substrate purposes. Right shape, but the radicalisation analogy is the wrong direction for Joe and worth thinking about why, because it sharpens what the kano moment can actually do in the book.
Radicalisation and the Andover-Harvard recognition both involve a self that had an identity strong enough to be shocked by its denial. The young Black man at Andover had built a self that included I belong here and the slur ruptures it because the self was real and the rupture is therefore traumatic. He has somewhere to go from the rupture — into rage, into withdrawal, into politics, into renewed work, into radicalisation. The rupture activates whatever the self was made of.
Joe does not have that self. Joe has a long-running performance of belonging propped up by a decade of small kindnesses. There is nothing underneath it. The kano moment does not rupture an identity; it reveals there was no identity to rupture. The vertigo is not the loss of a self; it is the recognition that the self he had been maintaining was a maintenance task, not a person. There is nothing to radicalise toward and nothing to defend. He cannot become angry at the speaker because the speaker was kind. He cannot retreat to a German identity because he has not been a German for thirty years and Hamburg is now also a place he does not belong to. He cannot return to the apartment with his mother because the apartment with his mother is also part of the same maintenance task. There is no exit register from the recognition.
This is why it functions as the second harbinger track and why it makes the death spiral structurally inevitable rather than dramatic. Drama would require somewhere for the shock to go. Joe has nowhere. The recognition lands and the recognition does not produce action; it produces accelerated drift. He drinks slightly more. He is slightly less present in the apartment. He stops greeting the corner vendor with the same warmth and does not know why. The vendor notices. The vendor adjusts. The relationship cools by one degree, which Joe interprets as evidence the kindness was always thin, which it might have been all along but might not have been until this moment, and now the recognition is producing the thing it claimed to have always been about. Self-fulfilling. The block becomes colder because Joe started reading it colder.
That is the death-spiral mechanism in its honest form. Not radicalisation. Not protest. Not even depression in the clinical sense. Just the slow withdrawal of one's hand from a relationship one has decided was never holding back, which causes the relationship to actually stop holding back, which confirms the decision. A man unmaking the conditions of his own life by ceasing to maintain the small acts that had been producing them.
The Hegel/Marx alienation reference is closer to the right register than radicalisation, but with a correction. The alienation those frameworks describe is alienation from a labour or a product or a self that the worker possessed in some prior state. Joe was never the owner of his belonging. He was a renter. The kano moment is the lease ending and his discovery that he had been calling it a deed. Alienation requires loss of property; eviction requires only the discovery that there was never a deed to begin with. Joe is being evicted, gently and without ceremony, from a place he had thought was his.
The right comparison is probably closer to certain late Greene moments — the Catholic priest who realises his priesthood was always a position other people had granted him conditionally, that he was a priest for them and never for himself, and that nothing remains when the granting stops. Greene knew this register because Catholicism trains you to think about identity-as-grant. Lutheran north Germany trains you to think about identity-as-discipline, which produces a different but related vertigo: if identity is something you maintain, and you stop maintaining, what is left is not a self but a maintenance log full of recent absences.
For the chapter outline this means the kano moment cannot be a single dramatic scene. It is a moment, yes, but the death spiral it triggers is not the consequence of the moment in the way drama treats consequence. It is the slow non-action that follows. Three or four chapters of progressively colder block-life, smaller interactions, slightly delayed greetings, the bartender pouring without being asked because Joe has stopped specifying. Each of these reads as continuity to a casual reader and as collapse to a careful one. The reader who is paying attention sees the temperature drop chapter by chapter; the reader who is not sees a man who has always lived this way and is just living it again. Both readings are correct. The careful reader's reading is the one the book is designed for, but the casual reader's reading is also true, because part of what the book is saying is that this kind of collapse is invisible while it happens, including to the man inside it.
Pair this with the mother's death and you have the structural midpoint engine. The kano moment can come before or after the mother dies, and the placement matters.
Before the mother's death: she is the last anchor that keeps the death spiral from accelerating fully, because his daily duty to her requires him to keep some maintenance running. The spiral is slow because she is still alive. When she dies, the maintenance has nothing to attach to and the spiral steepens.
After the mother's death: he is already drifting from the loss of the anchor when the kano moment lands. The recognition arrives into a man who has nothing left to absorb it. The death spiral was already underway; the kano moment is what makes it conscious, briefly, before the consciousness too dissolves.
I lean toward after, because the kano moment requires Joe to be slightly more porous than usual to register at all, and the post-mother grief opens the porousness. While she is alive he has too much daily structure to be punctured by a stranger's possessive. After she is gone there is gap enough for the small phrase to enter. If a friend does ask — and one might, because the bar is the bar — Joe says fine or changes the subject and the conversation moves on. The exchange is not the diagnosis. The exchange is more rash. The reader sees the deflection. The deflection is everything.
No structural device that signals significance. No chapter dedicated to the aftermath. No section break titled After. No epigraph that points the reader at what they are supposed to feel. The book does not do that work for the reader; the reader does it for themselves. If the reader does not do it, the reader was not the reader the book is for, and that is fine.
What this requires, technically, is brutally precise specificity in the rendered effects. The rash has to be visible and particular. He had not eaten that day is not enough by itself; he had not eaten because the kitchen had her things in it and he had not yet decided what to do with them would be too much, because the because is diagnosis. He had not eaten. The kitchen was the same as it had been. He went to the bar. That is the rash. Three sentences. The kitchen being the same as it had been carries the entire emotional weight because the reader knows what the same means after a death — the survivor has not moved anything because moving anything would acknowledge. The prose does not say that. The prose says the kitchen was the same. The reader supplies the rest.
This is also the discipline that protects against sentimentality. Sentimentality is what happens when the writer puts both the cause and the effect on the page, with the cause-and-effect linkage made explicit, so the reader is told what to feel and at what intensity. The withholding of cause forces the reader to construct the linkage themselves, and a linkage the reader constructs cannot be sentimental because it carries the reader's own interior into the construction. Sentimentality is impossible when the reader is the one supplying the emotional architecture. The book gives them the materials and refuses to assemble them.
It is also why the book can render genuinely unbearable material without becoming unbearable to read. The unbearable thing is not on the page. The effects of the unbearable thing are on the page. The reader can hold the effects because the reader is doing the holding actively, at their own rate, with their own interior resources. If the unbearable thing were named — he could not stop thinking about how she had died alone in the apartment whilst he was at the bar — the reader would either feel it imposed on them and recoil, or feel manipulated and disengage. He had not been home that night is a sentence the reader can hold. He had not been home and a chapter later the bar shut at four and a chapter after that he had been at the bar that night and he did not think about it now — those three sentences distributed across forty pages do the work that the named version would fail at.
The technical name for what we are describing is objective correlative, Eliot's term, though he did not invent the practice. Hemingway is its English-language theorist by demonstration. The iceberg principle in Death in the Afternoon: if a writer of prose knows enough of what he is writing about he may omit things that he knows and the reader, if the writer is writing truly enough, will have a feeling of those things as strongly as though the writer had stated them. Hemingway's caveat is the load-bearing one — if the writer is writing truly enough. The omission only works when what is omitted is fully known to the writer. The rash is precise because the diagnosis is precise off-page. Vague off-page diagnosis produces vague on-page rash, and the reader feels the vagueness as evasion rather than as discipline.
This is why the daughter-truth document is non-negotiable. The daughter has to be fully specified — every fact, every reason, every history — off the page so that the on-page renderings of her effects on Joe and on the world around her are precise. Same for the mother. Same for Mother Mary. Same for the wife. The book's whole register depends on the writer knowing things the reader will never read. The reader will feel the knowing through the precision of the omissions.

Joe should not go inside any of the establishments. Or — important variant — Joe should go inside one and not engage. He sits at the bar of one of the places. He drinks. He watches. The men around him are at a different stage of the same disease he is at, and the visibility of that stage is the scene's content. He is not aroused. He is not entertained. He does not name this to himself. The reader names it for him.

The function as you laid it out is exactly right and I want to confirm the discipline because the gimmick risk is real.
The flare functions as barometer — visible to the reader as a register of his interior weather, not narrated by him as such. He scratches. The skin breaks. He stops scratching when someone enters the room. He covers his hands with a long sleeve when the elbows are bad. He chooses which shirt to wear based on what shows. None of this is glossed. The reader sees the flare and registers what produced it; Joe does not connect the flare to the cause. This is the effects-only rule operating somatically. He had been at the bar for six hours and his hands were bleeding — full stop. The reader knows the bar produced the flare. Joe does not let the connection complete.
The flare functions as derailment — it interrupts. He cannot meet someone with his palms looking like that. He cancels something. He stays in. The skin keeps him from doing things and the things he does not do are part of the architecture of his life. This is also character. He has been derailed by his own body for years, in small ways, and the derailments have shaped what he has and has not done. Decisions he made for skin reasons he subsequently rationalised as decisions made for other reasons.
The flare functions as self-conscious trigger — he becomes aware of being looked at, in a register that is different from the rest of his self-awareness. The Kano Moment is a recognition of how he is being seen. The flare-awareness is a recognition of how his body is being seen. Both are about visibility. The flare provides a physical version of the same architecture the Kano moment provides socially. He hides his hands the way he hides his foreignness — except the hands are visible to him constantly, whilst the foreignness is invisible to him until someone names it. The two operate as parallel mechanisms of being-watched and being-revealed.
