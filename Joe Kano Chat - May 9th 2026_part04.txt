The man who blocks a number has the resources to find the number, locate the block function, execute the action, feel the small satisfaction of having done it. That is operational capacity Joe at this point in the book does not possess in any other domain. The drinking has eroded that kind of micro-decisiveness. He cannot click the cursor reliably; he cannot block a contact reliably either. The behaviour would not match the rest of his rendering.
The street-turn is worse because it is visibly avoidant in a way that requires him to register her presence, plan a response, execute the response, and live with having done it. Aggressive turning is performed avoidance. Performed avoidance is the behaviour of a man with enough remaining vanity to care that someone might see him be reached. Joe's vanity at this stage is in a different place — not too many calories, the partial discipline of the surrender. He is not maintaining the kind of vanity that turns visibly on a street. He is the man who would walk past her without seeing her, or see her and the seeing would not produce a decision, or see her and the decision would be made by inertia rather than by any active turning. The aggressive turn is from a younger and more energetic version of him.
The deeper problem with the hybrid: it gives the reader a moral read of his cut-off. He is being a coward. He is being unfair to her. He is hurting her by avoiding her. Those are reader-thoughts that activate when the avoidance is performed. The reader starts assessing him morally and the book loses the empathy floor it has been building. Whereas attrition-disappearance — he simply does not return, the days go by, the not-returning becomes the new fact — does not produce moral reader-thoughts. It produces sad reader-thoughts. The reader registers loss without registering blame. That is the register the book wants in the late chapters. Blame would tip the book into judgement, which it has refused throughout.
The Weltanschauung point you raised is the real diagnostic. The hybrid does not show us anything new about him. The German-wall version (the maintained refusal) at least reveals a structural feature — a north-German hardness that has been beneath the surface and which the reader had not seen activated. That is character data. The hybrid does not even give us that. Petty avoidance is not specifically German; it is universal small-spirited behaviour that any man of any background might exhibit. The hybrid spends a structural beat without earning anything.
The only argument I can construct for the hybrid is that a small petty gesture can sometimes function as a tell of how much the snap mattered to him — this is how much it cost him that he is now, briefly, the kind of man who blocks a number. The pettiness becomes evidence of the wound, not character in itself. The reader sees the smallness as proof of the largeness of what was lost. That is a defensible reading. But it requires the reader to do that interpretive work without prompting, and most readers will read pettiness as pettiness and stop there. The interpretive risk is too high. You cannot count on the reader to convert the small gesture into the inferred wound; many readers will just file him as a man who blocks numbers and turns on streets, and the empathy floor will crack.
So: hybrid out. Attrition-disappearance is the working choice with German-wall as the live alternative if the chapter outline produces room.

The No Country ending is the right reference whether you are a fan or not. Worth being precise about what makes it work, because it bears on what we are doing.
The Coen brothers' ending withholds the closure that the genre has trained the audience to expect. In a thriller, the antagonist gets caught or killed; in a Western, the lawman gets his moment. No Country refuses both. Bell sits at a kitchen table and tells his wife about a dream his father appeared in. Camera holds on his face. Cut to black. The audience walks out angry — the famous reaction in 2007 — because they have been trained to receive ending as resolution and the film has refused to deliver. The anger is the proof the choice worked. The film argued that the world it depicts does not have endings of the kind genre demands, and to provide one would be to falsify the world. The withholding is the statement. There is no other way to make the statement.
Cormac McCarthy did the same thing in the novel, slightly differently. Chigurh walks away from a car accident, injured but alive. The reader expects a final confrontation. There is no final confrontation. The novel ends on Bell's monologue. McCarthy and the Coens were both refusing the same thing for the same reason.
The French New Wave examples — 400 Blows ending on Antoine running to the sea and the freeze-frame on his face, Breathless with Belmondo dying in the street and Seberg's small qu'est-ce que c'est dégueulasse — operate by the same logic. Truffaut and Godard refused the resolution the studio film of their moment supplied as a contract obligation. The refusal was political as well as aesthetic. The studio ending was a lie about what life delivered; the New Wave ending was an honest statement that life does not, in fact, deliver.
Joe Cano operates in this lineage. The book has been refusing closures throughout — anecdote shapes refused, war stories cut before they resolve, harbingers dismissed by Joe and not converted into recognition by the prose, Mother Mary disappearing without coda. The ending of the book has to obey the same logic at the largest scale. The daughter is confirmed; the book ends; the reader does not learn what becomes of him, what becomes of her, whether they meet again, whether he tells anyone, whether he goes home and drinks differently, whether anything at all changes. The ending is the confirmation and a small additional thing — possibly a sentence, possibly a paragraph — and then the book stops.
The small additional thing is the technical question. No Country ends on Bell's father in the dream, riding ahead with the fire to make camp in the dark. That is not nothing. It is one image, freighted, that delivers the book's metaphysical position without resolving the plot. McCarthy chose to put something after the refusal of plot-closure because the something is the closure at a different level — the level of what the book has been about all along. The ending refuses the surface closure and provides the deep closure simultaneously.
For Joe Cano, the deep closure is not metaphysical in McCarthy's sense — the book is not a meditation on evil and aging. It is a portrait of a particular man in a particular ecology. The deep closure, if there is one, is something like: this is what a life of this kind looks like in its last moments of half-consciousness. Not a vision. Not a dream. Probably a small action, observed without comment. He puts the bottle down. He looks at the ceiling fan. He hears the morning prayer call from the church on the next block. He does not think anything. The chapter ends.
That is the working ending. Not locked. The actual final image is one of the things that gets discovered late in the writing rather than designed at the spine stage. But the shape of the ending — confirmation of the daughter as the structural climax, then a small held image, then nothing — is locked in by the principle you just articulated.
The facsimile of past observations note you opened with is honest and worth holding. The book is operating in a tradition. It will be compared to the books in that tradition. That is unavoidable and not something to defend against. What makes a book worth writing inside a tradition is not novelty of position but precision of execution. The Coens did not invent the unresolved ending. They executed it for that film with that material and the execution was earned. The French New Wave did not invent the open ending either; they took it from Italian neorealism and earned it for their own moment.
Joe Cano will earn its ending the same way — not by having a new ending but by having an ending that the specific accumulation of its specific 60-to-73 thousand words has made inevitable. The ending will read as the only possible ending if the rest of the book has done its work. If the rest of the book has not done its work, no clever ending will save it. The principle applies upstream: nothing in the late chapters can be designed for impact independent of what the early chapters have established. The withholding works because the giving was real. No Country withholds in the last act because the first two acts gave the audience a Western. Joe Cano withholds in the last chapter because the previous twenty-some chapters gave the reader a man.

What this rules out, concretely, is large categories of writing the book might otherwise be tempted by.
No interior monologue that processes an event after it has happened. He does not think about his mother in week three after the burial. He does not think about her in any chapter that comes later. The mother is gone from his thought in the same way she is gone from the apartment, which is to say the absence is the presence. The reader sees that he has not thought of her. The not-thinking is the rash. The diagnosis — because the relationship was unresolvable, because the funeral confirmed his exclusion, because the mourners knew her better than he did — stays off the page.
No flashback to clarify motivation. He does not remember the funeral in chapter twenty-two. He does not have a moment where the bell of a church reminds him of the bells at her burial and the prose lets the connection surface in his consciousness. The connection lives in the reader. He hears a bell and orders another drink. The reader hears the bell differently because the reader was at the funeral too. He was not. He was at the funeral on the page; he is not at the funeral in his memory; the prose does not intervene to bridge the gap.
No dialogue that explains. No friend asks how he is doing with his mother's death. If a friend does ask — and one might, because the bar is the bar — Joe says fine or changes the subject and the conversation moves on. The exchange is not the diagnosis. The exchange is more rash. The reader sees the deflection. The deflection is everything.
No structural device that signals significance. No chapter dedicated to the aftermath. No section break titled After. No epigraph that points the reader at what they are supposed to feel. The book does not do that work for the reader; the reader does it for themselves. If the reader does not do it, the reader was not the reader the book is for, and that is fine.
What this requires, technically, is brutally precise specificity in the rendered effects. The rash has to be visible and particular. He had not eaten that day is not enough by itself; he had not eaten because the kitchen had her things in it and he had not yet decided what to do with them would be too much, because the because is diagnosis. He had not eaten. The kitchen was the same as it had been. He went to the bar. That is the rash. Three sentences. The kitchen being the same as it had been carries the entire emotional weight because the reader knows what the same means after a death — the survivor has not moved anything because moving anything would acknowledge. The prose does not say that. The prose says the kitchen was the same. The reader supplies the rest.
This is also the discipline that protects against sentimentality. Sentimentality is what happens when the writer puts both the cause and the effect on the page, with the cause-and-effect linkage made explicit, so the reader is told what to feel and at what intensity. The withholding of cause forces the reader to construct the linkage themselves, and a linkage the reader constructs cannot be sentimental because it carries the reader's own interior into the construction. Sentimentality is impossible when the reader is the one supplying the emotional architecture. The book gives them the materials and refuses to assemble them.
It is also why the book can render genuinely unbearable material without becoming unbearable to read. The unbearable thing is not on the page.
