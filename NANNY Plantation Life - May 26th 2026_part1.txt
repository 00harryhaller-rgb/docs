Here is the research spine I [the chat, not me] would build the film world from. I’ll use “enslaved girl/woman” in the brief, though characters in the period would use harsher language.
1. The historical frame: Nanny’s Jamaica is a war zone inside a plantation society
For the Nanny period, think roughly early 1700s through the Maroon treaties of 1739–1740, with the story-world shaped by the First Maroon War, plantation expansion, runaway communities, and the constant British fear that enslaved people would escape, rebel, or aid the Maroons. Nanny is best treated as a historically grounded figure whose record mixes colonial documentation, oral history, and legend. Enslaved.org describes her as the central leader of the Windward Maroons in eastern Jamaica, while noting that her life is difficult to separate from later legend; it also places her in a Maroon community organized for concealment, ambush, and survival in mountainous country. 
The National Library of Jamaica gives the wider political frame: after the English captured Jamaica in 1655, enslaved Africans fled plantations and joined Maroon communities in inaccessible, forested mountain regions. The Maroons became expert at ambush, intelligence-gathering, and surviving in terrain the British struggled to control. The treaties of 1739–1740 recognized Maroon freedom and land, but also forced Maroon communities into the morally complicated role of helping suppress rebellions and return future runaways. 
That last point matters dramatically. Before the treaties, “Nanny” can be the name whispered as hope: the woman in the hills, the one who knows the way out. After the treaties, the Maroon world becomes more complicated: still free, still African-Jamaican, still feared by planters, but also bound by treaty obligations that could make runaways distrust them. If your film wants Nanny as liberator and mentor, the cleanest setting is before or during the treaty crisis, not long after.
2. Evidence problem: the sources are rich, but morally contaminated
There is a lot of information, but most of the surviving detail comes from enslavers, doctors, overseers, travelers, plantation records, and colonial law. That means the sources are useful for material life—work, food, punishment, houses, labor hierarchy—but they often distort enslaved people’s motives, voices, and inner lives. A good film should use those sources for the mechanics of the world, then restore interiority through character, oral tradition, music, memory, and community. Historians repeatedly warn that many early accounts of Jamaica were produced by white observers who obscured the centrality and brutality of enslaved labor. 
For texture, one of the most valuable later sources is the diary archive of Thomas Thistlewood, an English overseer and enslaver in Jamaica from 1748 to 1786. It is after Nanny’s main period, but close enough to illuminate plantation routines: work assignments, provisions, illness, death rates, punishments, purchases and sales, and sexual violence. Yale’s description of the Thistlewood papers explicitly notes their importance for understanding Jamaican plantation brutality and the culture of enslavement. 
So the method should be: use early laws and early travel accounts for Nanny’s generation; use mid-to-late eighteenth-century plantation records carefully as continuities, not one-to-one proof; use Maroon oral history and scholarship for Nanny’s world.
3. The plantation is not just a workplace. It is a coerced town with a command chain.
For screen purposes, the plantation should feel like a factory, prison, farm, village, military outpost, and market node all at once.
The white hierarchy usually runs like this:
Absentee owner: often in Britain, rarely seen.  NOT IN OUR CASE.  SOME OWNERS MAY SHOW UP WITH THE FRIGATE.  THAT COULD BE GOOD.  MASTER PRYCE GREETING NEIGHBOURS HE HASN’T SEEN FOR YEARS BOTH WITH CONTEMPT “you have no idea how what it is like here” AND CONCERN “they have the ear of the puppet-masters back in Grand Brittania”.
Attorney or estate agent: manages estates for absentee owners; may oversee several properties.
Overseer: the day-to-day white commander of the plantation.
Bookkeepers: young white clerks/supervisors, often brutal because they are ambitious, insecure, low-status, and trying to prove themselves.
White artisans, doctors, militia, jobbing men: people who enter the estate for repairs, punishment, medical treatment, slave-catching, or trade.
Glasgow’s slavery research describes this chain clearly: absentee owners relied on attorneys, overseers, and bookkeepers; bookkeepers were not merely clerks but supervised enslaved labor from dawn into the night, especially during sugar production, while Black drivers directed the gangs under white command. 
The enslaved hierarchy is just as important:
Drivers: enslaved men, and sometimes women, given coercive authority over work gangs. They are not free. They may protect people in small ways, but they also carry the whip and are under white pressure to produce results.
Sugar boilers, distillers, carpenters, coopers, masons, cattlemen, watchmen: skilled enslaved people with higher status and sometimes more mobility.
House servants: cooks, washerwomen, waiting boys/girls, maids, nurses. Higher status, better access to leftovers, clothes, gossip, and shelter—but also closer to white sexual violence and surveillance.
Field gangs: the majority. Hardest labor, lowest protection.
Small gang / grass gang: children, adolescents, older people, pregnant or weakened people, depending on estate practice.
Healers, midwives, nurses, “doctors” or “doctresses”: enslaved medical specialists, often respected by the enslaved community and used by the plantation when useful.
The social order on a plantation was not flat. One educational summary of Caribbean plantation records places white owners/managers and employees above skilled enslaved workers and house servants, with field workers lower in the hierarchy; it also notes that redeployment from house service to the field gang could be used as a severe punishment. 
For your prison-movie analogy, the closest “capo” equivalent is the driver, but do not make drivers simple villains. A driver might beat someone in public, warn them in private, falsify a work pace, hide a sick girl for one morning, or betray a runaway plot to save his own family. That moral pressure is the drama.
4. Where your 12–13-year-old girl fits
A 12- or 13-year-old girl is not a child in the modern sense inside this system. She is in the danger zone between childhood and full exploitation.
She might be:
•	in the small gang, weeding, carrying grass, scaring birds, gathering trash, carrying water, tending animals, bringing food, or doing light agricultural work; 
•	attached to an older woman as a helper; 
•	moved toward field labor if she is strong; 
•	selected for house service if she is considered useful, attractive, obedient, light-skinned, Creole-born, linguistically adaptable, or connected to someone already in the house; 
•	sexually noticed by white men, drivers, or older enslaved men, which makes this age especially dangerous. 
Diana Paton’s work on Jamaican plantation records gives an excellent model for adolescent labor. At Rozelle estate in St. Thomas, an enslaved woman named Phillis worked as a driver of a grass or small gang of children and teenagers aged roughly four to eighteen; other enslaved women cared for younger children so mothers could be forced into long workdays. 
That gives you a strong mentor structure. Before Nanny teaches the girl mountain survival, an older enslaved woman on the plantation can teach her plantation survival: when to look down, when to look up, which driver drinks, which bookkeeper likes to catch girls alone, which old woman knows roots, which house servant hears the white people’s plans, which market woman carries messages.
5. The working day: no clean clock, only bells, shadows, horns, and crop pressure
A sugar estate is not like a cotton field where the main image is picking. It is a brutal agro-industrial system. Enslaved people plant cane, weed it, manure it, cut it, cart it, crush it, boil the juice, cure the sugar, make molasses, and distill rum. Glasgow’s research describes Caribbean sugar plantations as both farms and factories, with enslaved men, women, and children working long days all year. 
The year has different terrors.
During planting and maintenance, people dig cane holes, carry manure, weed, repair ditches, clear land, cut grass, tend animals, carry water, and maintain provision grounds. Cane-holing could mean digging dozens of square holes in hard ground under surveillance, and manure baskets could be extremely heavy. 
During harvest and boiling season, the estate becomes hellishly mechanical. Cane must be cut, carted, crushed quickly, and boiled before the juice spoils. Glasgow’s account notes that during the boiling season, work could run on day and night shifts, and the mill was physically dangerous: if a hand or arm was caught in the rollers, the only way to save the person might be immediate amputation. 
For the film, make the work seasonal:
Morning: horn, bell, or shouted muster before full light. People emerge from huts. A girl learns who stands where. The driver counts bodies. Someone is missing. Everyone knows what that means.
Field day: the sun rises; the pace changes. The driver watches the line. A white bookkeeper rides past or stands with a stick. The girl learns to keep her hoe moving even when exhausted.
Midday: not rest in a humane sense. A pause for food, water, or repositioning. Gossip moves here. Warnings move here.
Afternoon: bodies slow. Punishments often come when output drops.
Evening: the plantation does not end. People cook, tend provision grounds, mend clothes, care for children, treat wounds, hide food, whisper news, sing, mourn, bargain, flirt, pray, plan.
Boiling season night: orange light from the boiling house, cane carts in the dark, men and women staggering between field and factory, the smell of sweet juice and burned flesh, a child falling asleep upright.
6. Where they slept: the enslaved village or yard
Early observer Hans Sloane described enslaved people’s houses in Jamaica as small thatched huts, usually set at some distance from the master’s house, with minimal possessions: mats for sleeping, an earthen pot for boiling staples, and calabash cups or spoons. He also observed people sleeping on mats made from rushes, plantain leaves, or similar materials, often with little covering. 
For the movie, avoid making the quarters look like tidy cabins. Think:
•	low huts, thatch, mud, wattle, rough timber; 
•	smoke trapped inside; 
•	a mat, a pot, a gourd, a bundle, maybe beads, a knife, a hidden charm, a scrap of cloth; 
•	fire as warmth, cooking, insect defense, and social center; 
•	too little privacy; 
•	children sleeping against older bodies; 
•	a sick woman coughing in the corner; 
•	a couple trying to maintain marriage under impossible conditions; 
•	someone always listening. 
The quarters are dangerous, but they are also where culture survives. This is where a girl learns songs, African words, insults, jokes, mourning customs, herbal knowledge, secret names, and who belongs to whom.
7. Food: not a cafeteria, but a forced subsistence economy
Do not stage food as a simple ration line. In Jamaica, enslaved people were often forced to feed themselves through provision grounds, small livestock, Sunday markets, and night cooking, supplemented by limited estate provisions such as salted fish.
Sloane observed enslaved Jamaicans eating staples such as yams, plantains, potatoes, maize, beans, and peas, with small amounts of salted fish; he described huts containing pots for boiling these foods. 
By the early eighteenth century, planters recognized that forcing enslaved people to cultivate their own food reduced the cost of feeding them. A Penn Museum article on Jamaican provision grounds notes that planters allowed enslaved people to grow food and sell surplus, often using Saturday afternoons and Sundays for cultivation and markets. 
This gives you one of the richest parts of the film:
A girl learns that survival depends on food politics.
She must know which provision ground belongs to whom. She must not steal from the wrong woman’s yam mound. She must know who shares pepper, who hoards salt, who can get fish, who waters down the pot, who hides a gourd of meal under ashes. Hunger is not just bodily; it is social.
The market woman or higgler is crucial. Enslaved women carrying goods to market moved along roads, carried messages, traded produce, watched troop movements, and spread news. One study of runaway women in Jamaica notes that many higglers were women, and that this work gave them unusually wide freedom of movement and knowledge of roads, plantations, and allies. 
For your Nanny story, a higgler is a perfect intelligence bridge between plantation and Maroon country. She can carry cassava, salt fish, gossip, gunflints, a charm, a warning, or a child.
8. House jobs: higher status, greater danger
A house job is not freedom. It is proximity to power.
House servants may get better scraps, old clothing, access to kitchens, news, and shelter from the worst field labor. But they are also under the eyes and hands of white men and mistresses. They can be beaten for a broken dish, blamed for theft, sexually coerced, used as spies, and punished by being sent to the field gang.
Evidence from plantation social hierarchies shows house servants were often considered higher status than field hands, and being sent from the house to the field could be punishment. The same source notes that lighter-skinned enslaved people, including children fathered by white men, were often given better positions. 
How does a girl get a house job?
Not by merit in the modern sense. She is selected.
She might be chosen because she is young enough to train, speaks English or Creole well, is considered “presentable,” is the daughter of a house woman, is light-skinned, has sewing or washing skill, can nurse a child, or has caught the eye of someone with power. That last possibility must be handled without coyness. Sexual exploitation of enslaved women by white men and supervisors was routine; the Thistlewood archive is notorious because he recorded sexual coercion and punishment in sickening detail. 
The house is a great dramatic location because it is where the girl can overhear:
•	troop movement against Maroons; 
•	a new law against runaways; 
•	the sale of a mother or brother; 
•	a planned punishment; 
•	a mistress’s jealousy; 
•	a bookkeeper’s fear; 
•	an overseer lying to the owner; 
•	the location of keys, passes, powder, knives, maps, or letters. 
Nanny’s lesson to the girl could be: “The field breaks your body. The house tries to steal your eyes. Watch everything. Want nothing they offer too quickly.”
9. Rules, passes, movement, and surveillance
Plantation discipline was backed by colonial law. Jamaica’s early slave laws empowered white authorities to punish, sell, chain, mutilate, execute, and otherwise control enslaved people, especially runaways or those accused of rebellion. The 1664 law, one of Jamaica’s first slave laws, explicitly framed enslaved labor as necessary to plantations and gave broad authority to punish and secure enslaved people. 
By 1717, Jamaican law targeted movement, assembly, gaming, drinking, drums, horns, and gatherings. The law punished runaways and allowed severe bodily punishment for those absent for extended periods. It also regulated free Black, mixed-race, and Indigenous people through certificates or badges, showing how deeply the colony feared unauthorized Black mobility. 
This is where your “rules of the prison” idea becomes historically powerful.
A young girl must learn:
Never be found on the road without a reason.
A basket, a message, a bundle of washing, a child, or a market errand can be camouflage.
Never speak a runaway’s name near the wrong person.
The person listening may have a child held hostage by the overseer.
Never assume the driver is only one thing.
He may be cruel at noon and save you at dusk.
Never assume the house is safer than the field.
In the house, the danger has doors.
Never let white people see all of your intelligence.
Looking stupid can be armor.
Never waste tears in front of people who trade in tears.
Grief is for the yard, the grave, the provision ground, the night.
Never trust quiet after a punishment.
Quiet is when names are being gathered.
Later Jamaican laws after Tacky’s Revolt in 1760 became even more explicit about restricting assemblies, weapons, and African-derived spiritual practices such as obeah. That is later than Nanny’s main period, so I would not import every detail backward, but it shows the same colonial fear: drums, horns, gatherings, healing, and spiritual authority were seen as political threats. 
10. Punishment: make the threat constant, not only spectacular
Sloane’s early account includes descriptions of whipping, mutilation, iron collars, restraints, and extreme punishments for running away or rebellion. His language is racist and apologetic, but the details are useful because they show how normalized terror was in plantation management. 
A film should not rely only on gore. The more frightening truth is the administrative normality of punishment.
Punishment can appear as:
•	a whip hanging where everyone can see it; 
•	salt or pepper mentioned before it is used; 
•	a girl counting scars on an older woman’s back; 
•	someone unable to raise an arm because of the mill; 
•	an iron collar heard before it is seen; 
•	a runaway forced to wear a sign, ring, chain, or bell; 
•	a driver ordered to punish his own kin; 
•	the silence after someone is sold. 
Also show the psychological economy. People are forced to watch punishments because punishment is theater. A young girl learns that crying too loudly can attract attention, but not crying can also be read as defiance.
11. The sick house or infirmary: not sanctuary, but negotiation
There was no modern “infirmary” in the humane sense. On larger estates, there might be a sick house, hot house, hospital room, or designated place where sick enslaved people were inspected, dosed, isolated, or returned to work. The surviving records often discuss sickness because illness affected labor capacity, not because managers respected enslaved health. The Thistlewood papers include extensive material on provisions, illness, death rates, work assignments, and punishment, which is exactly the kind of record that reveals plantation sickness as management. 
Enslaved medical workers were also part of the system. In eighteenth-century Jamaica, some enslaved people were identified in records as “Doctor,” “Doctress,” or nurse, and they treated other enslaved people while being incorporated into plantation medicine. Sasha Turner and other historians have shown how enslaved African healing knowledge was both used and marginalized by white medical authority. One Jamaican example from 1773 identifies an enslaved man named Galen as a doctor on a St. Ann estate. 
For story purposes, the girl should learn that sickness has rules:
•	If you look too healthy, you are accused of malingering. 
•	If you look too sick, you may be written off. 
•	If you go too often, you become marked. 
•	If you hide sickness too long, you collapse in the field and endanger the people beside you. 
•	The enslaved nurse may be kinder than the overseer, but she still has to report numbers. 
•	Some cures are official and useless; some are African and hidden; some are both. 
A good line for Nanny or the older mentor: “Do not go to the sick house empty-handed. Bring a witness, a symptom they can see, and a lie they already believe.”
That is dramatic reconstruction, but it fits the logic of the evidence.
12. Female-specific danger: work, sex, pregnancy, motherhood
Most enslaved women in the British Caribbean did hard agricultural labor. Paton notes that women formed roughly equal numbers on many Jamaican plantations and that the majority of enslaved women worked in manual agricultural labor. She also emphasizes the impossible conflict imposed on enslaved mothers, who were expected to sustain children while being forced into plantation labor. 
For a girl nearing puberty, the dangers multiply.
She must watch:
•	white overseers and bookkeepers; 
•	visiting doctors, artisans, militia, sailors, and buyers; 
•	drivers or favored enslaved men who abuse delegated power; 
•	mistresses who punish girls for the sexual violence committed by white men; 
•	older women who may protect her, but may also be unable to protect her; 
•	sale, pregnancy, disease, jealousy, and forced separation. 
Caribbean plantation records and summaries make clear that enslaved women were routinely raped or coerced by white men and supervisors; one source notes that some women were forced to use sexual access as a survival strategy under conditions they did not control. 
Do not write this as “romance with the master” or as a simple seduction plot. The power imbalance is the point. A young girl’s survival lesson is not “avoid desire”; it is learn who has the power to rename violence as favor.
13. Enslaved community politics: the rules inside the rules
The most important thing for your film is that the enslaved community is not a saintly chorus. It is a society under siege, with love, jealousy, hierarchy, betrayal, humor, beauty, and forced complicity.
The girl must learn the internal map:
The old women know births, deaths, herbs, grudges, names, and who was sold from where.
The driver knows the work pace, the overseer’s mood, and who is close to breaking.
The driveress or small-gang woman may control children’s labor and be both feared and maternal.
The house woman knows white secrets but may be resented for her relative privilege.
The cook controls scraps, fire, knives, and gossip.
The washerwoman sees blood, semen, sweat, theft, and disease on clothing.
The higgler knows roads and rumors.
The skilled man may have tools, mobility, and bargaining power.
The newly arrived African may know war, farming, spiritual systems, or languages others do not know, but may not yet understand plantation codes.
The Creole-born enslaved person may know English, estate rules, local roads, and how white people lie.
The healer may be trusted by the enslaved and feared by whites.
This is where the film can be subtle. Someone might betray a runaway not because they are evil, but because the overseer threatens their child. Someone might steal food because hunger has made morality thin. Someone might spread gossip to move attention away from a daughter. Someone might pretend to hate Nanny while secretly feeding Maroon scouts.
14. Nanny and the Maroon alternative
Nanny’s world should feel different from the plantation, not because it is easy, but because it is governed by different necessities: concealment, collective defense, mountain agriculture, spiritual authority, intelligence networks, and war discipline.
Enslaved.org describes Nanny Town as a community of perhaps 800 to 1,000 people in the first half of the eighteenth century, protected by terrain, concealment, misdirection, and ambush. It also notes the use of the abeng, the cow horn used for signaling, and describes Nanny’s role as both practical and spiritual: protecting the community and sustaining it. 
The plantation girl should first hear Nanny as rumor:
•	“She catches bullets.” 
•	“She lives where the white man’s horse breaks its leg.” 
•	“She sends the horn through the mountain.” 
•	“She knows when a girl is ready to run.” 
•	“She will not take a fool who brings soldiers behind her.” 
Then, when the girl reaches the Maroons, strip away some myth and deepen it. Nanny is not merely magical. She is disciplined, strategic, suspicious, maternal, and hard. Freedom in the mountains still means hunger, danger, rules, sentries, farming, raids, and fear of betrayal.
15. The cleanest story structure for accuracy
I would be careful about making Nanny simply “already on the plantation for years” unless you are intentionally creating a composite. Historically, Nanny is remembered primarily as a Maroon leader, not as a long-term plantation mentor. You have three cleaner options.
Option A: The historically strongest structure
The young girl is on a sugar estate. An older enslaved plantation woman teaches her the day-to-day survival rules. Nanny exists first as rumor, then as destination. The girl escapes and only later meets Nanny in the mountains.
This gives you two mentors:
•	Plantation mentor: teaches survival under slavery. 
•	Nanny: teaches survival in freedom and war. 
That is probably the best dramatic and historical solution.
Option B: Nanny as covert visitor
Nanny, or a woman acting under Nanny’s authority, enters the plantation world disguised as a higgler, healer, midwife, provision trader, or relative from another estate. She mentors the girl in fragments: a warning at the market, a charm in a calabash, a map sung as a work song, a test of silence.
This uses the documented mobility of enslaved market women and the known Maroon intelligence problem for the British. It gives you Nanny early without making her implausibly stationary. 
Option C: The poetic-license version
Nanny herself was once on that plantation and returns in memory, dream, or legend. This is less literal, but it can work if the film is openly mythic. The danger is that it may blur the political reality of Nanny as a Maroon leader. Use this only if the film’s style supports legend.
16. Specific “rules” Nanny or the older mentor can teach the girl
These are dramatized, but historically grounded:
“Learn the sound of every footstep.”
The overseer walks differently from the bookkeeper. The driver does not need to shout if everyone knows his rhythm.
“A house job is not a rescue.”
The house gives scraps, news, and cloth. It also gives locked doors.
“The woman with the basket knows more than the man with the gun.”
Market women and higglers move through the colony’s veins.
“Never steal from a poor woman’s ground unless you want the whole yard against you.”
Food is survival, property, pride, and kinship.
“When they ask who told you, say you dreamed it.”
Dreams, spirits, and rumor can protect a source.
“If you must cry, cry while carrying water.”
Water hides tears and gives you a reason to walk away.
“Do not run on the first day you are angry.”
Anger runs downhill. Escape requires roads, moon, food, silence, and someone who will not sell your name.
“The sick house is a door with teeth.”
Enter only when the lie, the symptom, and the witness agree.
“The driver’s whip has two ends.”
One cuts your back. The other is tied around his throat.
“If they ban the drum, it means the drum speaks.”
The colonial fear of drums, horns, gatherings, and African spiritual practice should be felt as proof of Black communication and power, not just repression. 
17. Scene palette
A few scenes that would give you the diurnal depth you want:
Opening plantation morning
A girl wakes on a mat before dawn. An older woman presses food into her hand without looking at her. Outside, the driver is already counting.
Small gang lesson
Children weed cane. The girl thinks this is “children’s work” until she sees the driveress strike a teenager for slowing down. The mentor tells her: “Small gang is where they teach your body to obey before it is strong enough to refuse.”
Kitchen temptation
The girl gets sent to the great house kitchen. There is meat, sugar, knives, and danger. She learns that hunger can make a trap look like mercy.
Sick house negotiation
A boy with fever is accused of pretending. The enslaved nurse knows he is sick but must choose her words carefully in front of the overseer.
Sunday market
Color, noise, bargaining, stolen glances, coded speech. A higgler gives the girl a route hidden in a song. White men see commerce; the enslaved see a communications network.
Punishment without spectacle
Do not show the whole whipping. Show everyone waiting for it. Show the girl hearing it while forced to keep stirring a pot. Show the old woman correcting her grip: “If it burns, they beat you too.”
Night in the yard
The girl hears three versions of Nanny: witch, queen, soldier. The mentor says none are enough.
First Maroon contact
No grand rescue. A birdcall answered wrong. A horn far away. A hand over the girl’s mouth. A test: “Who sent you?” If she says the wrong name, she is turned away.
18. What to avoid
Avoid making plantation life only a series of atrocities. The atrocities are real, but if every scene is only whipping, the audience learns less. The deeper horror is that people are forced to build ordinary life inside organized theft.
Avoid making the enslaved community morally simple. Survival creates compromise.
Avoid making Nanny a modern liberal heroine in costume. Make her strategic, spiritual, severe, funny when least expected, and unsentimental about mistakes.
Avoid treating Maroon freedom as paradise. It is freedom under siege.
Avoid importing later details without care. Some laws and records from the 1760s–1800s reveal continuities, but Nanny’s main period is earlier.
19. The core emotional engine
The girl enters believing survival means not being beaten.
The plantation mentor teaches her survival means reading power.
Nanny teaches her survival is not enough. Freedom requires discipline, memory, secrecy, land, weapons, food, and people willing to risk themselves for one another.
That is the movie’s deepest contrast: the plantation teaches her how to endure a world built to read her body as property; Nanny teaches her how to become unreadable.
