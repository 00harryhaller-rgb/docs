The Coen brothers' ending withholds the closure that the genre has trained the audience to expect. In a thriller, the antagonist gets caught or killed; in a Western, the lawman gets his moment. No Country refuses both. Bell sits at a kitchen table and tells his wife about a dream his father appeared in. Camera holds on his face. Cut to black. The audience walks out angry — the famous reaction in 2007 — because they have been trained to receive ending as resolution and the film has refused to deliver. The anger is the proof the choice worked. The film argued that the world it depicts does not have endings of the kind genre demands, and to provide one would be to falsify the world. The withholding is the statement. There is no other way to make the statement.

This is the discipline AQA was already doing in places. Reputation was never something you possessed. It was something other people temporarily granted. That sentence is the rash. The diagnosis — what produced the recognition, who taught it to the narrator, what specific betrayal made it knowable — is nowhere in the book. The reader carries the recognition without ever being told its provenance. The sentence works because the sentence is all there is. Joe Cano extends the same discipline to the entire architecture.
What this rules out, concretely, is large categories of writing the book might otherwise be tempted by.
No interior monologue that processes an event after it has happened. He does not think about his mother in week three after the burial. He does not think about her in any chapter that comes later. The mother is gone from his thought in the same way she is gone from the apartment, which is to say the absence is the presence. The reader sees that he has not thought of her. The not-thinking is the rash. The diagnosis — because the relationship was unresolvable, because the funeral confirmed his exclusion, because the mourners knew her better than he did — stays off the page.
No flashback to clarify motivation. He does not remember the funeral in chapter twenty-two. He does not have a moment where the bell of a church reminds him of the bells at her burial and the prose lets the connection surface in his consciousness. The connection lives in the reader. He hears a bell and orders another drink. The reader hears the bell differently because the reader was at the funeral too. He was not. He was at the funeral on the page; he is not at the funeral in his memory; the prose does not intervene to bridge the gap.
No dialogue that explains. No friend asks how he is doing with his mother's death. If a friend does ask — and one might, because the bar is the bar — Joe says fine or changes the subject and the conversation moves on. The exchange is not the diagnosis. The exchange is more rash. The reader sees the deflection. The deflection is everything.
No structural device that signals significance. No chapter dedicated to the aftermath. No section break titled After. No epigraph that points the reader at what they are supposed to feel. The book does not do that work for the reader; the reader does it for themselves. If the reader does not do it, the reader was not the reader the book is for, and that is fine.
What this requires, technically, is brutally precise specificity in the rendered effects. The rash has to be visible and particular. He had not eaten that day is not enough by itself; he had not eaten because the kitchen had her things in it and he had not yet decided what to do with them would be too much, because the because is diagnosis. He had not eaten. The kitchen was the same as it had been. He went to the bar. That is the rash. Three sentences. The kitchen being the same as it had been carries the entire emotional weight because the reader knows what the same means after a death — the survivor has not moved anything because moving anything would acknowledge. The prose does not say that. The prose says the kitchen was the same. The reader supplies the rest.
This is also the discipline that protects against sentimentality. Sentimentality is what happens when the writer puts both the cause and the effect on the page, with the cause-and-effect linkage made explicit, so the reader is told what to feel and at what intensity. The withholding of cause forces the reader to construct the linkage themselves, and a linkage the reader constructs cannot be sentimental because it carries the reader's own interior into the construction. Sentimentality is impossible when the reader is the one supplying the emotional architecture. The book gives them the materials and refuses to assemble them.
It is also why the book can render genuinely unbearable material without becoming unbearable to read. The unbearable thing is not on the page. The effects of the unbearable thing are on the page. The reader can hold the effects because the reader is doing the holding actively, at their own rate, with their own interior resources. If the unbearable thing were named — he could not stop thinking about how she had died alone in the apartment whilst he was at the bar — the reader would either feel it imposed on them and recoil, or feel manipulated and disengage. He had not been home that night is a sentence the reader can hold. He had not been home and a chapter later the bar shut at four and a chapter after that he had been at the bar that night and he did not think about it now — those three sentences distributed across forty pages do the work that the named version would fail at.
The technical name for what we are describing is objective correlative, Eliot's term, though he did not invent the practice. Hemingway is its English-language theorist by demonstration. The iceberg principle in Death in the Afternoon: if a writer of prose knows enough of what he is writing about he may omit things that he knows and the reader, if the writer is writing truly enough, will have a feeling of those things as strongly as though the writer had stated them. Hemingway's caveat is the load-bearing one — if the writer is writing truly enough. The omission only works when what is omitted is fully known to the writer. The rash is precise because the diagnosis is precise off-page. Vague off-page diagnosis produces vague on-page rash, and the reader feels the vagueness as evasion rather than as discipline.
This is why the daughter-truth document is non-negotiable. The daughter has to be fully specified — every fact, every reason, every history — off the page so that the on-page renderings of her effects on Joe and on the world around her are precise. Same for the mother. Same for Mother Mary. Same for the wife. The book's whole register depends on the writer knowing things the reader will never read. The reader will feel the knowing through the precision of the omissions.

If a chapter would be Joe sits in the apartment and thinks about his mother, the chapter does not exist. This will be the hardest discipline to hold in the writing because the temptation to give the reader the small interior gloss — half a sentence of clarification, one line of he remembered — will be constant. Every page will offer the temptation. The discipline is to refuse it every time. Trust the reader. Let them work. The effect-only rendering is the empathy mechanism the book has been building toward all along, and any breach of it cracks the floor.

Yes. And the distinction inside Joe's interior is the one that matters: he reflects in a register that is not the register the discipline rules out.
What we ruled out is the prose stepping in to gloss, to bridge cause and effect for the reader, to convert a felt rash into a named diagnosis. What we did not rule out — and what the book actively requires — is Joe thinking. Joe at three in the morning with the second bottle. Joe in the back of the car looking at the line at the gate. Joe washing his hands and noticing they have gone soft. Joe is thinking constantly. He has to be. A man with no interior on the page reads as a body, not a person, and the empathy claim collapses.
The distinction is what kind of thinking. His reflection is not analytical and not therapeutic. It does not produce understanding. It does not move from event to meaning to integration. It moves laterally, snags on small things, abandons threads, returns to the same memory three times in a chapter without progressing. The accreted register is itself the shape of his reflection. He thinks the way the prose thinks. The two are the same instrument.
What he does not think is the thing the reader is thinking about him. He does not think I have been a bad father or my mother withheld love or I drink because I cannot face. Those are the diagnoses the reader is constructing in parallel. The reader's diagnoses and Joe's reflections happen in the same chapters, on the same pages, sometimes in the same paragraphs, and they are different operations. The reader is doing analysis. Joe is doing something else — wandering, half-remembering, picking up a thought and putting it down again, registering a sensation without converting it to meaning.
Coetzee's Lurie does this. Lurie thinks constantly in Disgrace. Long stretches of interior. He has opinions, theories, fragments of poems, recollections of women, judgements of his daughter. The interior is dense. What he does not do is understand himself. The reader understands him; the reader assembles his condition from the texture of his thinking; Lurie himself is in the middle of his own life and cannot see what the reader sees. The interior is what makes the empathy possible. Without it, Lurie would be a man on a page rather than a consciousness the reader inhabits. With it — and with the discipline that the interior never explains itself — the book achieves the precise thing you just named. Craft becomes art when the interior is dense and uncomprehending simultaneously.
The invisible structure that delivers both effortlessly is the technical achievement. It looks effortless because the writer has done the work of separating the two operations cleanly. The reader's analytical work and the character's reflective work do not contaminate each other. The character's reflection does not slide into the reader's analysis (which would produce the diagnosis-on-page failure). The reader's analysis does not impose itself on the character's reflection (which would produce a character who is more articulate about himself than the condition allows). The two run in parallel, in the same prose, on the same page, and the reader experiences them as a single thing because the writer is delivering them as a single thing technically whilst keeping them separate operationally.
This is why the writer needs to know the diagnosis fully off-page. If the writer does not know what is wrong with Joe, the writer cannot keep Joe from almost knowing it on the page. A writer who is themselves working out Joe's condition as they write will have Joe arriving at insights, because the insights will be the writer's own emergent understanding leaking into the character. The discipline of Joe never understands himself requires the writer to understand him completely, in advance, so that the writing can render him as the man who is six steps short of every recognition the writer has already had.
The same applies to the supporting cast. Mother Mary's interior is fully knowable to the writer; the prose shows only what would be visible to Joe, which is small fractions of it. The mother's interior is fully knowable to the writer; the prose shows only what Joe permitted himself to see in her, which is less than the truth. The daughter's interior is fully knowable to the writer; the prose shows what Joe encounters in her, which arrives mostly as surface and posture and the small inflections that surface and posture carry. The reader assembles each of these characters from the fractions, and assembles them more accurately than Joe does, because the reader has the additional resource of not being Joe. The reader can see what he cannot.
This is also why Joe's reflection is the most interesting thing in the book even though it produces no insight. The reader is in his head whilst he fails to understand his own life, and the failure is intimate. The reader feels the texture of how he thinks — the snags, the small returns, the half-recognitions that fall away before completion, the moments where he almost notices something and the noticing dissolves. Each of those near-recognitions is a place where the reader sees the door he did not walk through, and the doors are everywhere in the prose, and the cumulative effect is the reader holding the book at the end and knowing things about Joe that Joe never knew. That is the empathy mechanism at full operation. The reader's knowledge of him is more complete than his own self-knowledge, and the gap between the two is where the love lives.
You are right that this is where craft becomes art. The technique is statable; the achievement is not. The technique is render the interior densely without letting it explain itself. The achievement is the reader leaves the book having loved a man who could not be loved by the people in the book. No amount of technique guarantees the achievement. The achievement is what happens when the technique is held precisely enough, page after page, that the reader's parallel construction of Joe accumulates into something the surface of the prose never names but the reader carries out of the book as their own possession.
