The discomfort you are circling is precise. Every interesting profession imports too much of itself into the prose; every drone profession reads as a tip-of-the-hat to existentialist tradition and converts the book into commentary on what it is doing rather than into doing it. Neither slot is right.
Let me push on a few of the candidates and then on the C++ proposal.
The interesting professions.
Helicopter pilot, distillery owner, chocolate-maker, film-industry industrial work — all carry the problem you identified. Each comes with its own ecology that the book would then have to render or refuse to render. Refusing to render the ecology while keeping the profession produces a strange void where the reader expects detail and the book withholds. Rendering the ecology imports a second world the book does not need. The cocktail-talk interest is the diagnostic — if a reader can ask what was that like about the profession itself, the profession is too loud for this book.
The pilot version specifically also has the wealthy-clients problem. If Joe was a private pilot for the wealthy, the book has a built-in entry into the Polo Club world that we have explicitly closed. That entry would re-open it through the back door. Out.
The distillery version has the alcohol-overlap problem you flagged — of course he is an alcoholic, he made alcohol. The reader assembles a causal narrative the book has been refusing.
The chocolate version is the most distinct from the alcoholism but is the most overtly quirky-protagonist signal. The reader files Joe as the Chocolate Guy. Wrong filing.
The passive-investor restaurant.
Your hesitation here is correct. The reasoning around why did he invest, what is his current involvement, what does the partner ask of him generates exposition the book does not want. Each clarifying answer adds a small backstory beat. The book accumulates beats that explain rather than render. Out.
Shipping / container / marine.

The shipping industry has expat presence — agents, surveyors, brokers, charter operators. Marine surveyor or cargo agent would not import a second world; the work is invisible to most readers and the book can leave it invisible without strain. But it carries its own problem: Joe would have a daily working life that the book would have to either render or omit. If he is currently working at it, the book has to decide what his work-day looks like. If he is retired from it, the book is in pension-territory which produces its own filing.
The drone option.
The reactionary problem you identified is the real one. Government drone or middle-management would read as Bukowski-with-a-different-postcode. Even if the book held the discipline against the homage, the reader would do the homage on the book's behalf. Out.
The C++ proposal.
Your instinct toward this is good and worth working through carefully because it almost-but-not-quite solves the problem.
The strengths: He is of the right age — early Old, late Middle — to have learned C++ in the late eighties / early nineties, which means he came in at the boundary between the older mainframe world and the modern object-oriented world. He represents a specific cohort who did the difficult learning before the languages got friendly. His skill is legacy maintenance, which is a real professional category — there are systems running on old code that no one wants to rebuild and that require occasional maintenance from the dwindling population of people who can read the code. This is invisible work. It does not import an ecology. The clients who hire him are abstract corporations or government offices. The work is solitary, mostly remote or near-remote. He bills by the hour or by the project. He probably has fewer clients than he did ten years ago. The work has been fading — younger coders have learned the older systems out of necessity, automated tools have replaced some of what he did, and his network of contacts has aged out alongside him.
