The blur-between-colours principle.
The framing you ended on is structurally important and worth naming as a memo principle in its own right. Not so isolatedly different that it looks like a prop. Not so overlapping that archetypes blur into each other. Each one a prop hitting one note alone is wrong; each one part of a spectrum where the blur between colours is exactly that — a blur, not a line.
This is the difference between a cast and an ecology. A cast is a list of distinct functions. An ecology is a continuous field with dense regions and sparse regions and gradual transitions. The book renders an ecology, not a cast. The clans are dense regions on a continuous field, not labelled boxes in a typology. A man from one clan can drift to another. A man can be in two clans simultaneously by attending two different bars. A clan can dissolve and reform. The boundaries are real but are not lines. They are the kind of boundaries that exist between sand and water on a beach — a zone, varying, observable, never crisp.
This is also a writing-discipline principle. When rendering any of the clans, the prose should not produce a clean type-rendering. The clan is a tendency, not a definition. The men in the clan are not all the same and the differences within are part of the texture. A weekend warrior who has been doing it for fifteen years is becoming a fallen angel. A nepo baby who keeps coming back longer than his peers is on the same trajectory. The book's geography is verb-shaped, not noun-shaped — what each man is doing, not what each man is. The reader assembles the typology from the verbs and the typology is not stated.

On the Fallen Angel as the only other one like Joe.

The case for: the book benefits from one other figure who does not perform in the bar's standard register. Joe-not-gossiping is one structural fact. If Joe is the only such figure, the not-gossiping reads as exceptional — Joe's particular condition, his depletion, his peculiarity. If there is one other man at the table with a parallel quietness, the not-gossiping becomes a configuration two men in the clan share, which is more interesting because it suggests the depletion has more than one route to it. Joe arrived at it through his arc; the Fallen Angel arrived through his fall; both end up at the same place. Two trajectories converging at the same silence. The case against: putting two non-performers at the same table risks under-populating the clan. The bar dynamic the book has been building depends on the constant verbal production of the others — gossip, rounds, claims, jokes, the rumours about Danger Mouse, the conquistador-castration-thesis articulations. If two of the four or five core members are quiet, the verbal weight shifts to the remaining loud ones and the book may need them louder than they should be, which costs realism.
The calibration that probably resolves it: the Fallen Angel is not gossiping in the same depleted register as Joe, but his quiet has different texture. Joe's quiet is depletion-of-energy. Joe's quiet has nothing underneath; the Fallen Angel's quiet has something specific underneath that he is keeping intact. Different mechanisms. Same surface effect: he is at the table and he is not contributing to the verbal apparatus.
This means the Fallen Angel is not Joe's mirror. He is a man who has kept something whilst losing everything else, and the keeping is one of the few things he still has. Joe has kept nothing. The first is that the Tucker Carlson / Megyn Kelly / fired-podcaster phenomenon is a current cultural type with a recognisable contemporary shape. The Fallen Angel resembles the type structurally — fired or self-exited from elite position, now performs critique of the institution he left, the critique functioning as exculpation. Same mechanism. But invoking the type, even implicitly, would tip the book into satirical or political register and the book has been disciplining itself against both. The reader who sees Tucker Carlson in the Fallen Angel is reading the wrong book. The book is not about that. The Fallen Angel happens to share architecture with that, but his architecture is older and more universal.
The second and larger thing: the Tucker-Megyn type is interesting only as cultural-political phenomenon. It does not have interior. It does not have pathos. It has only the public performance of grievance. The Fallen Angel is the opposite — he is the interior the public performance hides. The book is rendering what those people would be if you stripped the megaphone away and put them at a bar in Manila for a decade. The scarecrow they actually are, underneath the grievance-content. The book's contribution is getting at the substrate the public performance occludes. If the prose flicked toward the public performance — referenced political media, named the contemporary parallels, drew the connection — it would import the surface phenomenon back over the substrate the book is trying to render. The book would become about Tucker Carlson, in the worst possible way, by reducing its own deeper observation to the cultural-commentary version of itself.
Avoid means active discipline. Specifically.
The Fallen Angel's politics should not be contemporary American culture-war politics. He should not be quotable in the register of right-wing podcasters or left-wing twitter. His Marx is old Marx — actual reading, dense, philosophical, not the Marx that gets weaponised in 2026 discourse. He references Capital and possibly the Eighteenth Brumaire and Adorno and maybe Lukács. He does not reference contemporary leftist podcasters. He does not say things that could be retweeted. His political register is European intellectual of an older formation, not contemporary online combatant. This keeps him out of the cultural-commentary register the reader might otherwise file him in.
His Friedman-bashing should also be old-style — not the contemporary right-coded Friedman defence and contemporary left-coded Friedman attack, but the substantive economist's argument from a banker who actually had to engage with the Chicago school in his career and now disagrees with what he previously administered. He talks about Friedman the way a banker would, not the way a podcaster would. The texture is professional disillusionment, not ideological combat.
He does not have takes. He has positions. The distinction matters. Takes are short, pointed, designed for circulation. Positions are longer, more diffuse, embedded in argument. The Fallen Angel produces positions. Joe registers them as long-winded; the clan absorbs them as the lefty's noise; the reader registers them as the apparatus the man has built. None of this resembles the cultural-media version of the same political content.
He should also be quiet about Hollywood, the entertainment industry, journalism, and any other field that has produced the public-grievance figures of the 2020s. His domain was finance. He stays in finance. The bankers in the recognition scene are bankers, not media executives. The cleanness of his domain keeps him from accidentally rhyming with the public types. The reader who pattern-matches to Tucker Carlson would have to translate across industries to do so, which is friction, which mostly prevents the match.
Your point about telling indie filmmakers Hollywood does not care, focus on making better films is also relevant for what the book is doing. The Fallen Angel is exactly the figure who has not done that. He has not focused on making better anything in his current life. He has built an apparatus of critique and lives inside it, producing nothing. This is the deeper failure underneath the Tucker-style surface — the railing-against-the-system has replaced any positive production. The Fallen Angel does not work. He drinks and thinks and quotes Marx. Whatever creative or productive capacity he had as a banker has not been redirected to anything new. The scarecrow has the straws of his prior life rearranged, not converted into action.
This is the more devastating version of the type. The contemporary podcaster-figures at least produce content, however degraded. The Fallen Angel does not even produce content. He produces speech in the bar. He has reverse-engineered an ideology and uses it for nothing except the ideological work itself. He is the type without the platform. He is what the type would be if you removed the audience. The book's observation is therefore deeper than cultural commentary — it is an observation about what these men are when no one is watching. Which is what literature can render and journalism cannot.
Avoiding the commentary register also protects against the book aging poorly. Cultural commentary novels date faster than character novels because the cultural reference points shift quickly. A book that reads as about Tucker Carlson and his ilk in 2026 will read as quaint or confused in 2036. A book that renders the universal architecture of the man who has built an ideology to explain his fall and uses the ideology to avoid examining the fall reads forward indefinitely. Joe Cano is being written for a long readership, not for the moment. The Fallen Angel's substrate is permanent; the cultural-political surface is temporary. Stay underneath the surface.
One small writing consequence: the prose should resist any sentence that the reader could imagine being said by a 2026 podcast personality. If a Fallen Angel observation reads as something Tucker would say, cut it. Replace with something more textured, less quotable, less suited to soundbite. The clan does not retweet. Their conversations are not designed to circulate. Keep the Fallen Angel's speech in that register.
The discipline applies across the book, not just to him. The book has been holding it elsewhere already — Joe does not have political opinions in the contemporary sense, the bar does not discuss elections, the conquistador-castration thesis is presented as the men's own folklore rather than as positioned political content. The book operates underneath contemporary political discourse, which is what gives it whatever lasting interest it might have. The Fallen Angel must obey the same rule.
