Timeline.
The four-seasons jump version is wrong for this book and you have already half-diagnosed why. The chapter-titled-jumps mechanism imports a structural device that announces here is how the book is organised, and the announcement does work the prose should be doing. It is also the device most readers associate with novels-that-want-to-feel-significant. Cutting to Hot Season as a chapter heading is the literary equivalent of the lead-in-from-Tucker monologue — it tells the reader I am a serious book, observe my structure. The book has been refusing this register throughout.
The Stranger model — two parts, one major break — is the closer correct shape but Camus's break was for a specific reason (the murder, the trial). The book does not have an event of comparable architectural weight that would justify a hard two-part break. The mother's death is structurally important but is not a register-changing event the way the murder is in The Stranger. Joe's life after the mother is recognisably continuous with his life before. The seasons have not changed. The bar has not changed. The break would be artificial.
The right answer is seamless flow, time passing without announcement. Chapters end. Chapters begin. Sometimes a chapter ends Tuesday afternoon and the next chapter begins three weeks later in some other Tuesday afternoon. Sometimes the next chapter is the same evening. The reader registers time moving by accretion of small details — a maintenance fee invoice on the table, the maid mentioning a holiday, the mother's deterioration, the rains starting, the rains stopping. The book covers what it covers — probably eighteen months to two years — without ever telling the reader how much time has passed.
This is the harder discipline and is the one consistent with the rest of the architecture. The cool register and the accreted register both operate on attention to the present moment without temporal-architectural scaffolding. Adding part-breaks or section-headings would be the same kind of structural-device-as-content move that Faulkner uses with his multiple-narrator architecture. Joe Cano refuses it.
The reader has Camus and Coetzee and Greene and Endo as priors. None of them use part-breaks for time-passing in their tightest books. Disgrace runs continuously across roughly a year with no part-breaks; the reader feels the year passing through accumulation. The Power and the Glory runs across weeks to months without breaks; the priest's circuit is the structure, not chapter headings. Silence runs across years with seasonal markers embedded in the prose, not as chapter titles. Joe Cano follows this lineage. Time passes; the book does not announce its passing.
The practical mechanism: chapters are short (the 25-30 chapter, 2,200-2,800 word range we discussed). Each chapter is a unit of attention rather than a unit of plot. Some chapters cover an afternoon. Some cover a single conversation. Some cover three days. The book's pace is irregular and follows what the prose needs to render rather than what a clock would dictate. The reader registers irregular pacing as the texture of Joe's irregular life. He does not have a daily structure that produces uniform time-units. The book reflects the irregularity.
The harbinger architecture you locked depends on this. The first daughter rumour drops; a chapter or two later, life continues; weeks have passed; the second rumour drops without the reader being explicitly told weeks passed; the texture has signalled it. Hard part-breaks would force the harbingers into specific narrative positions ("Hot Season harbinger" / "Rainy Season harbinger"), which is less interesting than rumours arriving at irregular intervals across a continuous flow.

Half-aware in the priest's mode — Joe reaches the proposition, does not reach the application. The reader closes the gap. Used sparingly, the way Greene uses them. The hot-stove principle generalised to the sentence level.
The two classes have different disciplines. Class A figure rides inside its clause without being the clause's centre. Class B figure is often the sentence's centre — the observation IS the sentence — but the irony is buried, never announced, and Joe must never close the gap himself. Specifically: Joe cannot say to himself, even silently, "and that's me too" or any variant. If the prose lets him close the gap, the figure collapses into self-pity, which fails the empathy claim at 5.20.
The discipline that governs both classes is the same one you named: cannot transgress into self-awareness; cannot become poignant philosophy. The dog line works because the priest is losing hope while observing that humans lose hope, and the prose does not point. If Greene had written "the priest looked at the dog and recognised himself", the line collapses. The line works on the gap.
For Joe's book this is sharper than for Greene's because Joe has less interior than the priest does. The priest is articulate to himself; Joe is not. Joe's Class B figures should therefore be shorter, blunter, less philosophical than Greene's. The dog-line equivalent for Joe would not be a sentence about humans losing hope. It would be a sentence about, say, a man at the bar trying to remember someone's name and giving up — observed by Joe, who himself has been forgetting things for years and has no plan to fix it. The figure is in the equivalence. Joe sees the man. Joe does not see himself.
And the structural rule you just named — Joe may, at most, register "I should have learnt" in the moment, and that's all — is critical. It permits one small in-the-moment acknowledgment, but forbids any retrospective integration. He doesn't think back to the earlier observation. He doesn't draw the connection. The "I should have learnt" stays in the present, isolated, and dies there. The reader carries the connection. Joe is permitted to feel, briefly, that he has done something familiar. He is not permitted to know what.
That isolated-acknowledgment device is also Greene. It's also Highsmith. It's how the priest functions across Power and the Glory — repeatedly almost-recognising and never quite landing the recognition. The priest's apparatus, like Joe's, cannot route the recognition into integrated self-knowledge. The not-quite-landing is the architecture.

Both with Greene-of-Power-and-the-Glory and Highsmith-of-Tremor as positive reference, with the dog line cited specifically as type-specimen. The noir lineage stays named-and-not-consulted. The governing test (figure must arrive without the writer's trace) applies to both classes. The further test for Class B (Joe cannot close the gap; one in-the-moment "I should have learnt" permitted, no retrospective integration) applies to that class only.

Yes. Shadow-painting again — the technique that surfaced earlier in this chat. Define each by what the other is not, because the boundary is where the failure modes live.
The danger you're naming is real and specific. Class B is the more seductive of the two. A well-made ironic observation feels like literary achievement; a writer who has discovered it can produce one after another, and the prose develops a texture of revelation. Suddenly every sentence is doing double work, the reader is constantly closing gaps, and the book becomes a chain of small Greene-priest pronouncements where each observation arrives with its hidden second meaning. The book has stopped rendering Joe's apparatus and started performing its own cleverness about Joe's apparatus.
"Scaffold rot" is the exact word. The mechanism that was holding the building up has started growing through every floor.
The other direction has its failure mode too. A writer who has decided Class A is safer and Class B is risky will produce a book of small engineered Greene-Highsmith figures with no ironic-observation work at all, and the book loses its access to the priest-mechanism. The figured surface stays controlled but the deeper rendering Joe needs — observations that secretly describe him without him knowing — goes missing. The book becomes textured but uninterpretable at the level where Joe's blindness is the architecture.
So the memo amendment needs four explicit boundaries, two each:
For 5.22 (local figure / Class A):
What this is not. Not an ironic observation. The local figure does not have a hidden second referent that is Joe. It does not secretly describe its observer. It renders its actual subject (the building, the mould, the air, the body, the bar) and does only that. If a Class A figure starts to function as Class B — if the rendering of the building begins to feel like the rendering of Joe — the figure has slipped category and should be cut or rewritten.
What this is not, second. Not a noir-style figural texture. The figure does not announce itself, does not exceed its clause, does not become the sentence's reason. The figure is one tool among many; the prose chooses it occasionally for specific reasons. The prose does not have a relationship to figure that the reader can perceive.
For 5.23 (ironic observation / Class B):
What this is not. Not a local figure. Class B does not work by figural compression at the phrase level. It works by structural irony at the level of the whole observation. The observation is in clean prose, often unfigured — sometimes even bare declarative. Its work is in what the observation means about the observer, not in how it is figured. A Class B sentence with a Class A figure inside it is over-cooked; the irony and the figure compete for the reader's attention and both lose.
What this is not, second. Not poignant philosophy. The observation must not generalise. Greene's dog line gets away with the philosophical surface ("humans are the only animals capable of losing hope") because the priest would say it; the philosophical register is in his mouth. Joe does not have that register. Joe's Class B observations are blunter, more concrete, less aphoristic. "He couldn't remember his name" is more Joe than "memory fails everyone in the end". The first is observation. The second is poignant philosophy. The second is scaffold rot.
And then the governing rule above both: Class B requires Class A discipline as its floor. If the prose has been figuring constantly, the ironic observation lands on a base of figural noise and reads as another figure. The Class B mechanism needs the cool register as backdrop. The priest-line in Greene works because the prose around it is mostly unfigured. Highsmith's equivalent moments work for the same reason and even more so. The figured floor must stay clean enough that the ironic observation can be heard.
The ratios I'd propose, since rationing is the actual mechanism:
Class A — Greene's density in Power and the Glory is roughly one figure per page or slightly less. For Joe's book, less than Greene. Closer to Highsmith. One small figure every two or three pages on average, often longer stretches with none. The book's metabolic rate for figure is below Greene's, well below Lowry's, and orders of magnitude below noir's.
Class B — even rarer. A handful across the whole book. Six or eight, perhaps. Not one per chapter. Not every chapter. They land at moments where Joe's apparatus is doing something the reader needs to see, and where the ironic-observation device can carry the seeing. The hot-stove parallel scene in chapter 22 is one. The almost-better-man scene at chapter 26 might support another. The Mother Mary advice scene at chapter 25 — possibly. The funeral, possibly. The condo-as-object scene, possibly. Most chapters do not have one.
The test for whether Class B is over-used is the one you named: scaffold rot. If the reader starts to feel that every observation is doing double work, the book has tipped. The prose has converted its load-bearing mechanism into texture. Each individual instance might still be skilled; the cumulative effect is failure.
The amendment also needs one structural note that sits above both sections — let me call it the disclosed governing principle. Both mechanisms exist in service of Joe's opacity to himself. Class A renders the world Joe inhabits without making the world's rendering about him. Class B renders Joe's blindness by giving him observations he cannot turn on himself. Both protect the dense-uncomprehending-interior discipline. The moment either mechanism starts to threaten that discipline — Class A by becoming about the writer, Class B by becoming a series of authorial pronouncements about Joe — it has stopped serving its purpose.
