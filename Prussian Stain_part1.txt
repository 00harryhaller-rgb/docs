Direct verdict

Yes. Allied Control Council Law No. 46 was real. It was signed in Berlin on 25 February 1947 by the four occupying powers and formally dissolved the State of Prussia. Its preamble really did say that Prussia had “long been the bearer of militarism and reaction in Germany” and had “in reality ceased to exist.” Article I dissolved the Prussian state, its central government, and all subordinate authorities; Articles II–III transferred former Prussian territories, functions, assets, and liabilities into the new Länder structure.

The brutal correction is this: the law’s wording was politically loaded, historically reductive, and symbolically deliberate — but it was not pure invention. Prussia had a real militarised state tradition. But the statement collapses centuries of Prussian history into one post-war accusatory formula. It also ignores the fact that Weimar-era Prussia was one of the strongest democratic and anti-Nazi power centres in Germany before it was crippled in 1932 and then absorbed by the Nazi state.

So the answer is not “Prussia was secretly Nazi” or “Prussia was innocent and slandered.” The answer is: the Allies killed a mostly already-dead state and used Prussia as a symbolic container for the militarist, authoritarian, anti-democratic German tradition they wanted to eradicate.

What Law No. 46 actually did

The law was short. It did not abolish “Prussians” as people, nor could it erase Prussian culture. It abolished the State of Prussia as a legal-political entity.

The key language was:

“The State of Prussia, which has long been the bearer of militarism and reaction in Germany, has in reality ceased to exist.”

That was the official Allied wording. It was not a later myth. The law then declared Prussia dissolved and provided that former Prussian territory under Allied Control Council authority would either receive Land status or be incorporated into other Länder.

The U.S. State Department’s diplomatic record confirms that the Control Council approved and signed the law on 25 February 1947, and that the report section on “Liquidation of Prussia” consisted essentially of the law itself.

The Control Council was the four-power governing body for occupied Germany; decisions on Germany-wide matters had to be taken jointly by the four Commanders-in-Chief. That matters because Law No. 46 was not just a unilateral Soviet or French act. The United States, Britain, France, and the Soviet Union all signed it.

Why the Allies did it

The immediate Allied logic came from the post-war programme agreed at Potsdam: demilitarisation, denazification, decentralisation, and the prevention of future German aggression. The Potsdam Agreement explicitly stated that German militarism and Nazism were to be extirpated, that German armed forces and military traditions were to be dismantled, and that German political life was to be rebuilt on decentralised democratic lines.

Prussia was an obvious target within that logic because it was associated with:

the militarised Hohenzollern monarchy;
the officer corps and Junker aristocratic tradition;
the Prussian-led unification of Germany;
the dominance of Prussia inside the German Empire;
the authoritarian, centralised, bureaucratic image of German state power.

That association was not baseless. Frederick William I had given the Prussian state a distinct military-bureaucratic character, expanding and organising the army to an exceptional degree for the size of the population. Frederick the Great then used that military strength for aggressive expansion, especially in Silesia.

But here is the key: the Allied wording treated one major strand of Prussian history as if it were the whole of Prussian history. That is where the distortion lies.

The state was already largely dead before 1947

Law No. 46 was legally important, but it was not the moment when a fully functioning Prussian state suddenly vanished.

The collapse began earlier.

In 1932, Chancellor Franz von Papen used an emergency decree in the so-called Preußenschlag to remove the elected Prussian government led by Social Democrat Otto Braun. That action seriously weakened one of the last major republican bastions in Germany.

Then the Nazis completed the destruction of Prussian autonomy through Gleichschaltung. In 1934, the Nazi “Law on the Reconstruction of the Reich” abolished the sovereignty of the Länder and transferred it to the Reich. After that, Prussia existed more as an administrative shell than as a sovereign state.

By 1945, Prussia was also geographically broken. Large eastern Prussian territories — including East Prussia, Silesia, Pomerania, and eastern Brandenburg — were placed under Polish or Soviet administration after the war. Remaining western and central Prussian territories were folded into newly created or reorganised Länder.

So the sharpest formulation is this:

Law No. 46 did not kill a healthy Prussian state. It formally executed a state that had already been politically gutted by Papen, legally hollowed out by the Nazis, territorially shattered by defeat, and administratively superseded by the occupation order.

Was Prussia being branded as the “über-Nazi” version of Germany?

Not exactly. That formulation is too strong.

The law did not say “Prussians are Nazis.” It said the State of Prussia had been the bearer of militarism and reaction. That is a different claim.

But the broader symbolic move was real. Post-war Allied and German discourse often treated “Prussianism” as a root pathology: militarism, obedience, authoritarianism, caste privilege, officer-worship, and anti-democratic reaction. Historian John Zimmermann’s study of the post-war image of Prussia notes that after 1945 there was a clear connection made between Prussianism and Nazism, and that Churchill had described Prussian militarism as a root evil or disease. Zimmermann also notes that France especially pressed for Prussia’s destruction, while Britain and the Soviet Union had at times been more open to Prussia continuing as a Land.

So: yes, Prussia was turned into a negative symbol.
But: no, the precise claim “Prussians were officially branded as über-Nazis” is not supported by the law itself.

The more accurate claim is:

The Allies used Prussia as the legal-symbolic embodiment of the militarist and reactionary German tradition they wanted the post-war order to define itself against.

That is very close to the interpretation you floated, but it needs that distinction: the target was not every Prussian person; the target was the Prussian state tradition as the Allies understood it.

Was this proactively presented to the population?

Sourced answer: yes, to some extent. Not enough evidence here to call it a single massive propaganda campaign.

The law itself was promulgated publicly. The U.S. diplomatic record says the law was to be promulgated on 1 March 1947.

I also found evidence of public-facing occupation messaging. A U.S. Military Government Weekly Information Bulletin from March 1947 included an item titled “Abolition of the State of Prussia” that described Prussia’s development in terms of “inherent militaristic tendencies” and connected that trajectory to its formal abolition under Control Council Law No. 46.

That supports this limited conclusion:

There was official-public messaging that explained Prussia’s abolition through the lens of militarism.

What I would not honestly claim from the sources reviewed is that there was a proven, centrally choreographed Allied mass campaign to teach the population that ordinary Prussians were the “quintessential Nazis.” The evidence supports official legal-symbolic framing and occupation messaging, not that stronger claim.

The anti-Nazi Prussia point

You are right about one major thing: Prussia was not simply a Nazi heartland in the way the Allied formula implies.

The Weimar-era Free State of Prussia was governed for years by a democratic coalition and was often described as the “republican bulwark” of Germany. Its government under Otto Braun was one of the most important democratic stabilising forces in the Weimar Republic.

There were also conservative, aristocratic, military, and Christian opponents of Hitler whose backgrounds were bound up with old Prussian or east-Elbian elite worlds. The Kreisau Circle, centred around figures such as Helmuth James Graf von Moltke and Peter Graf Yorck von Wartenburg, brought together conservatives, Christians, liberals, and Social Democrats to plan a post-Nazi order. Some members were later linked to the July 20 resistance and were executed.

The July 20 plot itself involved both civilian and military officials, many of them conservative, nationalist, and aristocratic; key military conspirators included Friedrich Olbricht, Henning von Tresckow, Claus von Stauffenberg, and others.

But the hard correction is this: “Prussia was extremely anti-Nazi” is also too broad.

Some Prussian institutions, elites, officers, conservatives, aristocrats, and bureaucrats opposed Hitler. Others accommodated him, enabled him, served him, or shared enough authoritarian, anti-left, nationalist, anti-liberal assumptions that they helped make the Nazi seizure of power possible. The July 20 resistance itself was morally mixed: some conspirators opposed Nazi crimes; some acted because the war was lost; some were implicated in criminal policy. The U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum explicitly notes that motivations varied and that some conspirators were themselves implicated in war crimes or the Holocaust.

So the clean version is:

Prussia was not Nazi by essence. Prussia was not anti-Nazi by essence either. Prussia contained democratic, authoritarian, militarist, reformist, Protestant, aristocratic, bureaucratic, Enlightenment, conservative, liberal, and resistance traditions. Law No. 46 flattened that complexity into a post-war political judgement.

The Von Steuben / U.S. military point

The transcription seems to mean “Prussians,” not “Russians.”

Here the correction is necessary: Baron von Steuben did not “create the U.S. military” by himself, and Prussia as a state did not create it. That is overstated.

The accurate version is stronger because it is defensible: a Prussian officer, Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben, played a decisive role in professionalising and training Washington’s Continental Army at Valley Forge. The National Park Service states that Steuben trained Washington’s troops at Valley Forge and helped create a more effective, professional Continental Army; the National Museum of the United States Army likewise credits him with transforming the Continental Army into a more professional and effective force.

So: Prussian military expertise mattered enormously to the American Revolution.
But: the U.S. military was not simply “created by Prussia.”

Was the Allied judgement historically fair?

Partly. But as written, it was crude.

The fair part: Prussia really did have a powerful militarist-authoritarian tradition. The army, bureaucracy, monarchy, Junker landed elite, and officer corps were central to Prussian identity and state development. The Allies did not invent that from nothing.

The unfair or reductive part: Prussia was not only that. It also included legal reform, administrative competence, religious toleration in certain periods, Enlightenment currents, educational reform, disciplined civil service traditions, and later a major democratic republican government under Weimar. Treating Prussia as “since time immemorial the bearer of militarism and reaction” was a polemical judgement, not a balanced historical analysis.

The most academically defensible verdict is:

Law No. 46 was legally real, politically understandable, symbolically intentional, and historically oversimplified.

What the dissolution really achieved

It achieved three things.

First, it removed any possibility that Prussia could re-emerge as a dominant German Land. This served the Allied policy of decentralising Germany and preventing a return to strong centralised militarist power.

Second, it helped legitimate the new federal order. Former Prussian territories were reorganised into Länder or absorbed into other Länder. The Bundestag’s research service notes that after 1947, the remaining former Prussian territories were integrated into newly formed Länder, which became legal successors for former Prussian territorial functions.

Third, it created a symbolic rupture. Post-war Germany — both West Germany and East Germany — did not present itself as Prussia’s successor. Later commentary notes that “everything Prussian” was widely discredited after 1945, and that neither the Federal Republic nor the GDR defined itself as Prussia’s heir.

That is the deeper implication: Prussia was made into the anti-model. The post-war order defined itself against militarism, authoritarianism, aggressive nationalism, and centralised state power; Prussia was used as the historical emblem of those things.

The “700-year culture” issue

This needs precision.

Prussian history can be traced back to the medieval Prussian lands and the Teutonic Order’s conquest in the thirteenth century, while the Kingdom of Prussia dates from 1701 and the Free State of Prussia from 1918. Britannica distinguishes these meanings: medieval Prussia, the Hohenzollern kingdom, and the post-1918 Land abolished by the Allies in 1947.

So saying the Allies dissolved a 700-year culture is emotionally understandable but legally inaccurate.

They dissolved the modern Prussian state, not the entire historical-cultural inheritance. But in practical terms, the blow was enormous because the war, expulsions, border changes, occupation policy, and abolition of Prussia together shattered the territorial and institutional continuity of Prussian identity.

The best formulation:

Law No. 46 did not erase seven centuries of Prussian history, but it intentionally ended Prussia as a political name, legal framework, and legitimate state identity.

Final judgement

The claim that Allied Control Council Law No. 46 dissolved Prussia and branded it as a bearer of militarism and reaction is true.

The claim that Prussia was thereby made into a symbol of everything the post-war order wanted to reject is broadly true.

The claim that ordinary Prussians were officially branded as über-Nazis is too strong. The official target was the Prussian state and its alleged militarist-reactionary tradition, not every person of Prussian origin.

The claim that Prussia was simply anti-Nazi is also too strong. Weimar Prussia was a democratic anti-Nazi bulwark; many Prussian-linked conservatives and officers resisted Hitler; but other Prussian-linked elites enabled, served, or overlapped with the authoritarian forces that made Nazism possible.

The cleanest conclusion is this:

Prussia was posthumously convicted in 1947 for the militarist-authoritarian tradition associated with it. The conviction had evidence behind it, but the sentence was written in the language of political symbolism, not historical nuance.
