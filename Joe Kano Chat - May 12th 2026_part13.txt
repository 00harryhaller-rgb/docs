I went back to look. Part Four braids two grammars (cool/parataxis + accreted), but both registers as defined are observational. The cool register names things flat. The accreted register catches and unspools — but on what is, not on what something is like. The Camus-positive diagnostic at 5.17 explicitly refuses metaphysical inflation. That tilt was right for the Faulkner-of-Absalom refusal — refuse the building, refuse the ping, refuse dynastic time. It is wrong as a blanket refusal of metaphor. We collapsed two things that needed to stay separate: structural metaphor (Absalom's edifice, the architecture of meaning piled up sentence by sentence) versus local metaphor (the simile, the figurative phrase, the rendered drip). The memo's refusals are aimed at the first; it has been implicitly forbidding the second as collateral. That is the mistake.
Manila does beg this. The humidity, the canals, the mould in the corridor, the gates, the way light works in that climate — these are conditions that the cool register can name flat but cannot make do work on the reader's body. A pure cool-register Manila is a wire-frame Manila. The reader gets the proposition of the city but does not get the city. Same with the bar at five p.m., same with the apartment at four when the mother used to be visible, same with the funeral. Without local figuration the prose has handed the reader observations it cannot make land.
Noir is the right reference because noir solves exactly this problem. Chandler, Hammett, Ross Macdonald (the writer, not the typographer — and the coincidence of names is funny). They wrote about depleted men in damp cities with apparatuses on the edge of failing. They used metaphor constantly. Their metaphors were hard. Not lyrical lift. Not romantic gilding. The figure was load-bearing and slightly mean. "A blonde to make a bishop kick a hole in a stained-glass window" works because it is engineered, not poured. The metaphor stays in the world's economy. It does not transcend the world; it renders the world by acknowledging that observational naming alone cannot get there.
That's the discipline we want. Not Lowry's metaphor (lifts the prose toward myth), but Chandler's (drops the prose deeper into the substrate). Two different uses of figure entirely. The memo's refusal of Lowry should stand. The implicit refusal of Chandler should be lifted.
The other reference that belongs here is Greene. Greene used metaphor. Power and the Glory is full of it. The whisky priest's apparatus, the heat, the police lieutenant, the cantina — these arrive in figured prose, and the figure is what makes the depletion register as depletion rather than as a proposition about depletion. We already had Greene in the writers' room at 14.2 and didn't notice we were borrowing his structural disciplines while quietly forbidding his sentence-level technique.
Highsmith too. Tremor of Forgery is cooler than Greene, but it is not metaphor-free. The Tunisian heat does work figuratively at moments. The book stays cool because the figures are rationed and unshowy.
So the correction is: rationed, hard, world-internal figure. The cool register holds as the metronome. The accreted register handles the unspoolings. But within both, local metaphor is permitted and in some places required — because without it Manila does not get rendered, it gets diagrammed.
Three working rules I'd add to the memo:
First, no figure that lifts the prose toward myth, redemption, or transcendence. Lowry-side metaphor stays refused. The figure must stay in the world the prose has built. If it gestures past that world, it fails.
Second, the figure should be engineered, not poured. Chandler's simile feels constructed; Lowry's metaphor feels overflowing. Construction is the disposition. The writer made this. The figure carries the writer's choice, not the prose's overflow.
