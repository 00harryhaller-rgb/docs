Yes, and harbingers he dismisses or overlooks is the precise mechanism — not the prose hinting to the reader past him, but him receiving the information and discarding it, with the reader watching the discard happen.
The distinction matters because it's the difference between dramatic irony and indictment. Dramatic irony is when the reader knows something the character doesn't. That's a cheap engine and reads as authorial winking. Indictment is when the character has been given the information, repeatedly, and has chosen each time not to let it land. The reader isn't ahead of him because of access. The reader is ahead of him because the reader is paying attention and he isn't. That's a moral position, not a narrative trick.
Practical guidance for the harbingers, since you'll be designing them in the outline:
They have to be the kind of information he could plausibly dismiss. Not crude — not someone walking up and saying that girl looks like you. That's a beat he'd have to actively suppress and the suppression would register to him as suppression. The harbingers need to be the sort of thing his existing worldview disposes of automatically. A kuya who mentions a sister working in town. An old acquaintance who says I heard about your situation and he assumes she means his drinking or his money or his ex-wife and changes the subject. A photograph at someone's house he doesn't look at twice. The information arrives inside frames he already has answers to, so the answer fires before the information lands.
Vary the source. If all the harbingers come from women he's slept with or women in the bars, his dismissal pattern is monotone — these people lie about men like me. If one comes from another expat, one from a Filipino man he respects, one from a child, one from someone with no stake at all, then the dismissal mechanism has to work differently each time, and the reader sees a worldview that's not just defensive but systematically closed. The frame isn't women lie; the frame is I already know what's true. Which is the larger indictment.
Don't let any single harbinger be the one that should have broken through. If there's a should-have-known moment that's clearly more obvious than the others, the finale becomes about that moment specifically and the indictment narrows to a single failure. Keep them all roughly equivalent in legibility. The cumulative weight is the point — no single one was definitive, and that's the whole problem. He had a dozen chances and the worldview disposed of all of them at the same automatic level. The reader counts them silently.
The last harbinger before the confirmation is the trickiest one. Too close to the finale and it reads as final-act setup. Too far and the rhythm dies. My instinct: the last one should arrive in a scene that is otherwise about something else — a scene with completely different stakes that the reader is invested in for its own reasons, and the harbinger drops in as background detail. The reader is watching the foreground; the harbinger registers in peripheral vision; he misses it because he's also watching the foreground. That's the most damning of all of them because the reader can't even fault him for missing it without fauling themselves for nearly missing it too.

Every joke in the bar must read as something Joe and his friends say to each other when no narrator is present. Not a joke the writer is constructing for the reader's amusement and putting in their mouths. The moment a joke smells of being for the reader, the war-story patina is back, even if the joke isn't about damage. The patina is not about content; it is about address. Who is this for. If the answer is the reader, the joke breaks the room. If the answer is the table, the reader laughs and stays inside the room.
This sounds abstract. In practice it means: the jokes are mostly small, mostly timing-based, mostly inside the group's verbal economy. The so far as I know harbinger we just discussed is exactly this. So is teamwork works read straight by the man who painted it on the wall. So is the Filipino joke about the foreigner who drinks Emperador, told to the foreigner's face by the bartender, and the foreigner laughing because the alternative is admitting it stings. So is one expat correcting another's pronunciation of a Tagalog word the first one is also getting wrong. The humour is competence-of-the-group humour. It rewards the reader for having paid attention. It does not perform.
The other test: the humour cannot be the writer's humour. This is the harder one for you specifically, because you have a real comic instrument — the dad jokes, the Witzelsucht, the porpoise, the dime-stretching-to-Porsche. Those are yours. Joe does not have access to them. Joe's humour is duller, more reflexive, more reliant on group-tic and ritualised line. Joe says so far as I know because every man at the table has said it; he does not invent the joke. The writer can invent the joke and put it in someone else's mouth — Oliver, the bartender, a child overheard — but Joe's interior comic register has to stay inside the limits of his own intelligence and his own degraded inventiveness. A man in late-stage chronic alcoholism is not a witty man. He laughs at jokes he has heard before. He repeats lines other people fed him last week. The book's discipline is to keep Joe's humour at the level his life has reduced it to, even though the writer could give him better.
This is connected to the spillage into mayhem you flagged. Some do spill into mayhem. Yes. That is the Nil by Mouth move precisely. Ray's pub stories start funny and end with him at his wife's throat. The shape of the joke does not change; the temperature does. The same anecdote-rhythm that produced laughter at minute three produces violence at minute seven, and the audience's memory of having laughed minutes ago is part of what makes the violence land. The audience was with the table when the table was funny; the audience cannot dissociate when the table turns. Complicity locked in by the laughter.
For Joe Cano, this means the humour scenes and the harm scenes are not different scenes. They are the same scene at different points along the curve. The bar night that begins as banter slides through an hour of drinking into something colder, and the prose tracks the slide without announcing it. A line that would have been a joke at 9pm reads as menace at 1am because the room has rotted in the meantime. Same line. Different temperature. The reader feels the shift before the prose names it, which is the only honest way for the prose to render it.
The exhale function you described — a sweet treat that gives the reader a break whilst also giving them a little gift for wading through the muck — is real and necessary. Without it the book becomes a slog and the reader's empathy turns into duty. But the exhale has to come from inside the room, not from the writer reaching in to pat the reader on the head. The exhale is the moment the men in the bar genuinely amuse each other and the reader is included by proximity rather than by performance. Brief. Specific. Often trailing off into something that makes the warmth retroactively unstable. He laughed and then he could not remember what he had been laughing at. That kind of trailing-off keeps the exhale honest. Pure laughter without aftertaste reads as relief; laughter that fades into not-quite-remembering reads as a man.
One concrete suggestion for the chapter outline phase, since this is the kind of texture that gets lost when one outlines structurally: mark the comic register of each chapter explicitly. Cool with one black laugh. Warm in middle, soured by close. Pure menace, no exhale. Comic throughout, the chapter that lets the reader breathe. Treat humour as a structural element with its own pacing curve across the book, not as ornament that arrives where it arrives. The reader needs the breath chapters specifically because the airless ones are working hard. Compose the breathing.
Barfly is a useful reference too, but watch it carefully on rewatch. Bukowski's humour in Barfly is closer to the line we are trying not to cross — the bar fights have a stylised quality that flirts with myth-of-the-drunk, the camera adores Mickey Rourke a little too much, the dialogue occasionally signals to the audience. Where Barfly works for Joe Cano is in the daily-ness — the rhythm of routine drinking, the small economies of who buys whose round, the bartender as confidant — but where it tips into romance is exactly what we are watching for. Use Nil by Mouth as the upper register of the model, not Barfly. Barfly is the temptation. Nil by Mouth is the discipline.
The mother addition from the last ramble pairs with this perfectly, by the way. Mother-care scenes are quiet by structural necessity; the reader cannot be in the bar for the mother chapters, and the mother chapters create the silences in which the post-death harbingers can land. Humour in the mother chapters is different humour — drier, more reflexive, the kind a tired man uses to manage his own pity. He told her the joke about the doctor and she laughed and forgot it within a sentence. That kind of thing. The bar humour and the mother humour are different instruments and the book uses both. The contrast itself is one of the things the prose tracks.
Add Nil by Mouth to the writer's room as the eleventh seat. Or unseat someone. Probably unseat Bolaño, whose value here was always more about scale than register, and you have decided this is not a scale book. Oldman earns the chair because he solved your central problem on screen and the solution is studyable scene by scene.
