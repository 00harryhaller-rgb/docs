Timeline.
The four-seasons jump version is wrong for this book and you have already half-diagnosed why. The chapter-titled-jumps mechanism imports a structural device that announces here is how the book is organised, and the announcement does work the prose should be doing. It is also the device most readers associate with novels-that-want-to-feel-significant. Cutting to Hot Season as a chapter heading is the literary equivalent of the lead-in-from-Tucker monologue — it tells the reader I am a serious book, observe my structure. The book has been refusing this register throughout.
The Stranger model — two parts, one major break — is the closer correct shape but Camus's break was for a specific reason (the murder, the trial). The book does not have an event of comparable architectural weight that would justify a hard two-part break. The mother's death is structurally important but is not a register-changing event the way the murder is in The Stranger. Joe's life after the mother is recognisably continuous with his life before. The seasons have not changed. The bar has not changed. The break would be artificial.
The right answer is seamless flow, time passing without announcement. Chapters end. Chapters begin. Sometimes a chapter ends Tuesday afternoon and the next chapter begins three weeks later in some other Tuesday afternoon. Sometimes the next chapter is the same evening. The reader registers time moving by accretion of small details — a maintenance fee invoice on the table, the maid mentioning a holiday, the mother's deterioration, the rains starting, the rains stopping. The book covers what it covers — probably eighteen months to two years — without ever telling the reader how much time has passed.
This is the harder discipline and is the one consistent with the rest of the architecture. The cool register and the accreted register both operate on attention to the present moment without temporal-architectural scaffolding. Adding part-breaks or section-headings would be the same kind of structural-device-as-content move that Faulkner uses with his multiple-narrator architecture. Joe Cano refuses it.
The reader has Camus and Coetzee and Greene and Endo as priors. None of them use part-breaks for time-passing in their tightest books. Disgrace runs continuously across roughly a year with no part-breaks; the reader feels the year passing through accumulation. The Power and the Glory runs across weeks to months without breaks; the priest's circuit is the structure, not chapter headings. Silence runs across years with seasonal markers embedded in the prose, not as chapter titles. Joe Cano follows this lineage. Time passes; the book does not announce its passing.
The practical mechanism: chapters are short (the 25-30 chapter, 2,200-2,800 word range we discussed). Each chapter is a unit of attention rather than a unit of plot. Some chapters cover an afternoon. Some cover a single conversation. Some cover three days. The book's pace is irregular and follows what the prose needs to render rather than what a clock would dictate. The reader registers irregular pacing as the texture of Joe's irregular life. He does not have a daily structure that produces uniform time-units. The book reflects the irregularity.

The first daughter rumour drops; a chapter or two later, life continues; weeks have passed; the second rumour drops without the reader being explicitly told weeks passed; the texture has signalled it. My preference: muscle-memory reflection, two or three glancing instances across the book, never named, never explained.
Reasoning.
None loses something the architecture wants. The fiancee was the load-bearing event that produced the man who arrived in Manila. If the book contains no trace of her, the cause-of-Joe is invisible in a way that costs a small amount of texture. The reader feels Joe's depletion but does not have the shadow of what produced it before the book began. The book has been distributing the cause-of-Joe across multiple substrates — mother, body, BPO arc, depletion-as-condition — and the fiancee is one of those substrates. Removing her entirely makes the distribution slightly thinner.
More — recurring explicit references, multiple chapters with her in his interior, possibly a flashback — would violate the no-flashback discipline and would convert her into a named loss in the reader's filing. The reader would file her as the woman he loved and lost and the filing is wrong. She is not the lost love. She is the woman who almost destroyed him before she ended it. The book cannot afford the standard filing.
Muscle-memory reflection — two or three glancing instances — gives the architecture what it needs without overweighting her. The instances would be:
One — a bodily reaction to a particular voice or posture in the bar. A woman speaks in a way that activates something below the level of articulation. Joe's hand moves to his glass. His shoulders lower one increment. The reaction is registered without being named. The reader sees the reaction and registers that something has been touched. Joe does not register what was touched. The instance lasts a sentence.
Two — a moment of refusing a configuration that the reader retroactively understands as fiancee-shaped. A woman approaches him in the bar in a particular register — not predatory, possibly genuine, possibly something he could have responded to. He does not respond. He turns slightly away. The non-response is rendered as Joe's standard depleted register but the prose can pitch it slightly enough that the reader feels this is also the thing he learned not to walk into. One small moment. No explanation.
Three — a thought-fragment that surfaces and dissipates. He is walking. He passes some triggering small detail. A thought begins. In Bucharest she had — The thought stops. He keeps walking. The unfinished thought is the entire instance. The reader has Bucharest as a one-word reference and assembles the rest from nothing.
Three instances across the book is enough. Each is short — two to four sentences at most. None names her. None explains. The reader carries her as an absence-shaped presence. The architecture has the cause-of-Joe distributed across mother, body, BPO arc, fiancee, and Manila itself, and each contributes its weight without the others overshadowing it.
This is the right calibration for the structural function she serves. The book gets the airtight motivation for his arrival in Manila and the residue-of-her in his interior, without converting her into a named character or a flashback subject.
The Bucharest naming as a one-word thought-fragment is also useful because it gives the reader a placement without giving them content. The reader knows Joe was in Bucharest. The reader does not know why or for how long or with whom or what happened. The single word produces an enormous amount of unspecified backstory in one register the reader can hold.
