The effects of the unbearable thing are on the page. The reader can hold the effects because the reader is doing the holding actively, at their own rate, with their own interior resources. If the unbearable thing were named — he could not stop thinking about how she had died alone in the apartment whilst he was at the bar — the reader would either feel it imposed on them and recoil, or feel manipulated and disengage. He had not been home that night is a sentence the reader can hold. He had not been home and a chapter later the bar shut at four and a chapter after that he had been at the bar that night and he did not think about it now — those three sentences distributed across forty pages do the work that the named version would fail at.
The technical name for what we are describing is objective correlative, Eliot's term, though he did not invent the practice. Hemingway is its English-language theorist by demonstration. The iceberg principle in Death in the Afternoon: if a writer of prose knows enough of what he is writing about he may omit things that he knows and the reader, if the writer is writing truly enough, will have a feeling of those things as strongly as though the writer had stated them. Hemingway's caveat is the load-bearing one — if the writer is writing truly enough. The omission only works when what is omitted is fully known to the writer. The rash is precise because the diagnosis is precise off-page. Vague off-page diagnosis produces vague on-page rash, and the reader feels the vagueness as evasion rather than as discipline.
This is why the daughter-truth document is non-negotiable. The daughter has to be fully specified — every fact, every reason, every history — off the page so that the on-page renderings of her effects on Joe and on the world around her are precise. The reader will feel the knowing through the precision of the omissions.

The flare functions as barometer — visible to the reader as a register of his interior weather, not narrated by him as such. He scratches. The skin breaks. He stops scratching when someone enters the room. He covers his hands with a long sleeve when the elbows are bad. He chooses which shirt to wear based on what shows. None of this is glossed. He had been at the bar for six hours and his hands were bleeding — full stop. The reader knows the bar produced the flare. Joe does not let the connection complete.
The flare functions as derailment — it interrupts. He cannot meet someone with his palms looking like that. He cancels something. He stays in. The skin keeps him from doing things and the things he does not do are part of the architecture of his life. This is also character. He has been derailed by his own body for years, in small ways, and the derailments have shaped what he has and has not done. Decisions he made for skin reasons he subsequently rationalised as decisions made for other reasons.
The flare functions as self-conscious trigger — he becomes aware of being looked at, in a register that is different from the rest of his self-awareness. The Kano Moment is a recognition of how he is being seen. The flare-awareness is a recognition of how his body is being seen. Both are about visibility. The flare provides a physical version of the same architecture the Kano moment provides socially. He hides his hands the way he hides his foreignness — except the hands are visible to him constantly, whilst the foreignness is invisible to him until someone names it. The two operate as parallel mechanisms of being-watched and being-revealed.
Use it as primary. The flare is a recurring visible fact across the book. Three or four times across the manuscript he is aware of his skin in a way that interrupts something. The OCD question.
No. Trust your instinct. Joe should not have your OCD.
Two reasons. One, OCD as written character-trait imports a register the book has decided against — the quirky-condition register, the protagonist-with-a-specific-syndrome register, the checking-locks-three-times visual shorthand. Even if you write it without the cliches, the reader brings the cliches. The book does not need that filing cabinet activated.
Two, and more importantly, OCD operates on a different psychic architecture than what Joe carries. OCD is a disease of over-control — the sufferer is trying to manage anxiety through ritual, repetition, certainty. Joe's condition is under-control — he has surrendered most discriminations and most rituals and most certainties. Giving him OCD makes him a man whose interior contains a competing structure that should be doing some work; the book then has to either render the work being done (which makes him more interior-ordered than the rest of the book wants him to be) or render the work failing (which is plot the book does not want). Either way, it costs more than it earns.
The Cymbalta question is similar but slightly different. He could be on something — a sleeping pill, a blood pressure medication that interacts badly with alcohol, the leftover bottle of something prescribed years ago that he still takes because he started. The pharmacy bottle on the bedside table is its own piece of texture. He took something with the brandy. He had been taking it with the brandy for years. That is the daily-medical reality of a man like him. The specific drug does not need to be antidepressant; in fact antidepressant imports the same syndrome-register as OCD would. Better if it is something more banal. Heart medication. Statins. A sleep aid he has been on for a decade and which has stopped working. The accumulation of small medications adds to the texture without becoming character.
