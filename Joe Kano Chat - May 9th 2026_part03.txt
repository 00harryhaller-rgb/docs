You will write a scene of a hand failing to find a mouse-cursor and the reader will read it and will not know why it lands the way it does. It will land because it is true at the level of bodily detail, and embodied truth in fiction is the thing that cannot be faked and cannot be missed.
That said: this is also exactly the material the discipline we set up was designed to protect. Anecdote-as-moral-structure was the warning. I was in the ER three times with a .412, .523, .711 wants to be told. It is precisely the kind of detail that, told the wrong way, converts harm into spectacle. The Belfort failure mode. So let me be specific about how I think this material gets in.
Joe does not narrate his drinking history. Not in monologue, not in flashback recounting, not in confession. The numbers and the milestones are yours; Joe lives inside the texture they produced. His hands shake in the morning. He keeps nips in his jacket because the bottle in the freezer is too far. He cannot click the cursor where he wants it. He vomits bile in a sink and rinses it without thinking. He counts calories on dark Coke Zero whilst not counting calories on the brandy. None of this requires the reader to know how bad it has been quantitatively. The reader knows from the texture. He could not get the cursor to settle and then he could and then he could not — that sentence in the cool register, mid-page, with no surrounding explanation, is more devastating than any ER scene.
The ER scenes themselves probably do not go in. They are too narratively shaped, too quotable, too easy for the reader to file under intense. The book is not interested in intensity. It is interested in the daily.
The Ross material is the harder question. Ross is not Joe. Ross is what Joe is becoming. The Cape Cod men who Humpty-Dumpty'd themselves into shells are Joe's American mirror, the version of him that did not even bother with the expat trajectory — they shattered in place. There is real risk in Ross becoming the book's tribute, the dedication-page presence that breaks register because the writer cannot help it. So a question for you, not now but eventually: does Ross belong in the book at all, or does he belong to you. Some material is fuel without being content. The fact that the writer knows what Ross became is what produces the prose; Ross himself does not need to walk through the Manila bar. Sit with this. I am not telling you to keep him out. I am asking you to interrogate what he is doing if he is in.
On the responsibility frame.
The Aristotle line — drunk, you are doubly responsible because you got drunk and chose to and you did the action — is the moral floor under the book. That floor cannot be Joe's floor. Joe does not have it. Joe's floor is more like I did not ask for this and the pain made me. The writer's floor and Joe's floor are different floors and the gap between them is one of the things the book is for. You will be tempted, in close moments, to give Joe your understanding. Resist. Joe gets your knowledge of what it feels like but not your knowledge of what one owes for it. The first is empathy. The second is wisdom. The book is being written by a man with both; it is being inhabited by a man with neither.
This is the load-bearing reason the book can be empathic without being sentimental. The writer has earned the right to write Joe by having survived a version of him; the writer has not earned the right to redeem Joe, because Joe has not earned that himself. The Costa Rica clinic is yours. Joe does not get one.
On the mother.
Yes. Bring her in.
You are right that Oliver's actual brother — curbstomped, catatonic, lobotomised — is too much. Not because the violence is too extreme but because the character cannot do enough work for the page-real-estate his presence costs. He would arrive as gothic, and gothic is the wrong register. The mother does work. The mother is structural.
What you are describing — invalid mother in Manila, hates the place, hates the son for being her caretaker, performs sweetness for outsiders, was abusive when she had strength — gives Joe four things at once.
First, it gives him a reason to be in Manila that is not pure self-indulgence. He is a dutiful son. The book does not let him off the hook for the rest of his life on this basis, but the duty is real and the duty constrains him. A man with a sick mother in the next room cannot collapse fully because the collapse would have to look like something to her. The mother is one of the few pressures keeping his apparatus running. This makes the apparatus visible by giving it something to push against.
Second, it gives the reader something to like about Joe. Not redeem — like. The reader needs occasional handholds of this man is, in this small way, doing something honourable. Without those handholds the reader bails. Greene knew this. The whisky priest in The Power and the Glory keeps offering the sacraments. Coetzee's Lurie tends to dying dogs. Joe takes care of his mother. The handhold is small and partial and does not exempt him from anything else, but it keeps the reader in the room. The book needs that.
Third — and this is the structural masterstroke if you can hold the discipline — the mother who hates him without ever saying so is the original architecture of every relationship he has with women. The performative sweetness toward outsiders. The withheld love that arrives only as containment. The contempt visible only to him and only when no one else is in the room. He has been training for transactional intimacy his whole life because his first intimacy was already transactional. He gives care and is paid in something that almost looks like love. He learned this script before he could speak it. The book does not have to say any of this. The reader sees the mother and sees Joe with the women and the reader does the work. Her mother's love did not arrive as hatred. It arrived as containment — your own line from AQA, applied here in a different gender register, would land hard.
Fourth: she dies. Mid-book or two-thirds in. Not as event-in-itself but as the removal of the last responsible anchor. The book gets quieter for a chapter and then gets worse. The man with nothing left to be responsible to is a dangerous man, yes — but more precisely, a man with no remaining external check on his collapse. The drinking, which had a ceiling because someone needed him sober some of the time, loses the ceiling. The ecology, which had a counterweight in the daily duty, loses the counterweight. He drifts further and the harbingers about the daughter start landing with no maternal-care container to interrupt them.
This also lets the daughter-rumour and the mother's death potentially overlap or sequence, which gives you compositional choices: a man who hears the rumour whilst burying his mother (the news-as-burden, dismissed because he cannot hold another weight) versus a man who hears it after she is gone (the news-as-freedom, dismissed because there is no one to be accountable to anymore). Either is interesting. They produce different books. Worth noting and deciding later.
The discipline you named — the dutiful son who does not complain, the absence speaks for us mode of rendering the lack of love — is exactly right. Do not write the mother as a villain. Write her as a woman whose face changes when only Joe is in the room. Write her sweetness to the doctor and Joe registering it without commenting on it. Write the small cruelties as small. The reader will understand. The reader always understands when the writing trusts them. Hammered, it becomes melodrama. Restrained, it becomes the architecture of his life.
Two warnings.
One: the temptation will be to make the mother explain Joe. He is what he is because of her. Refuse. The mother is one factor among many; she is also a person; her cruelty is not a key that unlocks Joe and absolves him. Joe is responsible for what he does. The mother is part of why he became someone who does it; she is not the reason. Hold the difference. Otherwise the book becomes a Freudian thesis and stops being a portrait.
Two: do not over-research her into a clinical category. Resist diagnosing her on the page. The reader can decide whether she is a personality disorder, a wartime survivor whose harshness is generational, a woman simply embittered by life, or something else entirely. The book does not need the answer. The book needs the texture.
Add her to the memo as a major supporting character when you record next, or I will when you tell me to. She is more important than I would have thought before this ramble.
Take care of yourself. Six months is the period when people start to feel solid and the ground hasn't fully cured underneath them yet. The book is good work but the book is also a book, and the book can wait if a day needs to be a day. Memento mori paired with and tomorrow is the line.

Claude responded: Nil by Mouth is the right reference and the right kind of right.
Nil by Mouth is the right reference and the right kind of right. Not for the milieu — Deptford council estate is not Manila — but for what Oldman did at the level of register that we have been circling.
The thing he got right that most British realism gets wrong: the distance. Loach and Leigh hold their characters at the political middle-distance, where structure is visible but interiority is rationed. Nil by Mouth gets in too close for that. Nick James's review nails it — Brechtian distanciation supplanted by an intimate relationship to the film subject. The viewer is confined inside the room with these people. No exit aperture. No analytical handhold. Which is exactly what we have been calling restraint as the empathy mechanism: the reader cannot get out, and there is no theatrical distance to hide behind, so complicity becomes structural. Oldman did it with camera and proximity. You will do it with syntax. Same operation.
And — directly relevant to the humour question — the other thing Oldman got right: these people are funny. The pub scenes have real laughter in them. Ray's monologues are, before they curdle, performances his friends actually enjoy. Val and Janet have moments of genuine warmth. The film does not let the humour be ironic-funny or audience-superior-funny; it lets it be the humour these people would actually laugh at, which the audience is therefore allowed to laugh at without becoming a tourist. That permission — the audience laughs with them, not at them, and not above them — is the technical achievement.
You are right that this is paramount. Let me sharpen what makes the move work versus what makes it fail, because the line is thinner than it looks.
The humour has to be the humour the characters would produce whether or not they were being watched. That is the test. Every joke in the bar must read as something Joe and his friends say to each other when no narrator is present. Not a joke the writer is constructing for the reader's amusement and putting in their mouths. The moment a joke smells of being for the reader, the war-story patina is back, even if the joke isn't about damage. The patina is not about content; it is about address. Who is this for. If the answer is the reader, the joke breaks the room. If the answer is the table, the reader laughs and stays inside the room.
This sounds abstract. In practice it means: the jokes are mostly small, mostly timing-based, mostly inside the group's verbal economy. The so far as I know harbinger we just discussed is exactly this. So is teamwork works read straight by the man who painted it on the wall. So is the Filipino joke about the foreigner who drinks Emperador, told to the foreigner's face by the bartender, and the foreigner laughing because the alternative is admitting it stings. So is one expat correcting another's pronunciation of a Tagalog word the first one is also getting wrong. The humour is competence-of-the-group humour. It rewards the reader for having paid attention. It does not perform.
The other test: the humour cannot be the writer's humour. This is the harder one for you specifically, because you have a real comic instrument — the dad jokes, the Witzelsucht, the porpoise, the dime-stretching-to-Porsche. Those are yours. Joe does not have access to them. Joe's humour is duller, more reflexive, more reliant on group-tic and ritualised line. Joe says so far as I know because every man at the table has said it; he does not invent the joke. The writer can invent the joke and put it in someone else's mouth — Oliver, the bartender, a child overheard — but Joe's interior comic register has to stay inside the limits of his own intelligence and his own degraded inventiveness. A man in late-stage chronic alcoholism is not a witty man. He laughs at jokes he has heard before. He repeats lines other people fed him last week. The book's discipline is to keep Joe's humour at the level his life has reduced it to, even though the writer could give him better.
This is connected to the spillage into mayhem you flagged. Some do spill into mayhem. Yes. That is the Nil by Mouth move precisely. Ray's pub stories start funny and end with him at his wife's throat. The shape of the joke does not change; the temperature does. The same anecdote-rhythm that produced laughter at minute three produces violence at minute seven, and the audience's memory of having laughed minutes ago is part of what makes the violence land. The audience was with the table when the table was funny; the audience cannot dissociate when the table turns. Complicity locked in by the laughter.
For Joe Cano, this means the humour scenes and the harm scenes are not different scenes. They are the same scene at different points along the curve. The bar night that begins as banter slides through an hour of drinking into something colder, and the prose tracks the slide without announcing it. A line that would have been a joke at 9pm reads as menace at 1am because the room has rotted in the meantime. Same line. Different temperature. The reader feels the shift before the prose names it, which is the only honest way for the prose to render it.
The exhale function you described — a sweet treat that gives the reader a break whilst also giving them a little gift for wading through the muck — is real and necessary. Without it the book becomes a slog and the reader's empathy turns into duty. But the exhale has to come from inside the room, not from the writer reaching in to pat the reader on the head. The exhale is the moment the men in the bar genuinely amuse each other and the reader is included by proximity rather than by performance. Brief. Specific. Often trailing off into something that makes the warmth retroactively unstable. He laughed and then he could not remember what he had been laughing at. That kind of trailing-off keeps the exhale honest. Pure laughter without aftertaste reads as relief; laughter that fades into not-quite-remembering reads as a man.
One concrete suggestion for the chapter outline phase, since this is the kind of texture that gets lost when one outlines structurally: mark the comic register of each chapter explicitly. Cool with one black laugh. Warm in middle, soured by close. Pure menace, no exhale. Comic throughout, the chapter that lets the reader breathe. Treat humour as a structural element with its own pacing curve across the book, not as ornament that arrives where it arrives. The reader needs the breath chapters specifically because the airless ones are working hard. Compose the breathing.
Barfly is a useful reference too, but watch it carefully on rewatch. Bukowski's humour in Barfly is closer to the line we are trying not to cross — the bar fights have a stylised quality that flirts with myth-of-the-drunk, the camera adores Mickey Rourke a little too much, the dialogue occasionally signals to the audience. Where Barfly works for Joe Cano is in the daily-ness — the rhythm of routine drinking, the small economies of who buys whose round, the bartender as confidant — but where it tips into romance is exactly what we are watching for. Use Nil by Mouth as the upper register of the model, not Barfly. Barfly is the temptation. Nil by Mouth is the discipline.

The contrast itself is one of the things the prose tracks.

The structural move is exactly right and you have just diagnosed why the Cuba/Brazil/Costa Rica triangulation does not fully transfer to Manila for Joe specifically. You could pass in those places because the genetic substrate of those populations includes you. The Filipino genetic substrate does not include a North German. He is visible at every distance. The premise of his self-illusion is therefore different from yours and structurally weaker — he has no plausible deniability with his own face. Which makes the illusion he maintains more interesting, not less, because he has built it against more contradictory evidence.
What he has substituted for passing is belonging. Decade-plus in the same flat. Same bartender, same vendor at the corner, same neighbours. They greet him by name. They ask after his mother. They know his orders before he places them. He has confused this — being known — with being one of them, and the confusion is entirely sustained by the courtesy of people who have decided he is no threat and is good for the small economy of the block. The illusion is not his alone; it is co-produced. Everyone is being polite. Politeness over a decade reads, to a man who needs it to, as inclusion. He does not realise the politeness is the ceiling, not the floor.
Then the line lands. You will always be Joe. From someone who likes him. Said affectionately or absently or in a small shared joke that the speaker does not register as containing what it contains. The speaker is not telling Joe off; the speaker is naming the obvious. Joe has been kano for ten years and is kano now and will be kano the day he dies in the apartment. The neighbours saying his name kindly were not naming him; they were calling him by the only name available. He had heard kindness; he had not heard the boundary inside the kindness.
This pairs structurally with the daughter-rumour mechanism in a precise way. Both are social truths delivered inside speech that the speaker does not weight as truth-delivery. Both are dismissed by Joe via existing frames he has built specifically to let him not hear them. Pikot dismisses the rumour. They love me here dismisses the kano line. Both frames are doing the same job — letting him keep the version of himself he has constructed over a decade — and both fail at the same time, in the last movement, when the structures all give at once.
It also gives you a second harbinger track running underneath the daughter track. Not just you have a child you do not see but you have a place you do not actually have. Both are versions of you have not been here in the way you thought you were. One is genealogical; one is geographic-social. They pair like two voices in the same fugue and the resolution at the end is the same chord struck on both at once.
The mechanism for the kano-line harbinger is best if it is even quieter than the daughter ones. The daughter rumours have a verbal shape that catches attention even when dismissed. The kano moments are atmospheric — a pause when he enters somewhere new with one of his neighbours, and the neighbour quickly explains him to the room as kano namin, our kano, with the possessive that he hears as belonging and which the room hears as classification. Their kano. Like a dog or a recipe. He does not catch it. The reader catches the possessive and the hairline fracture inside it.
A few of these across the book. Quiet. Spaced. Each dismissed by him as kindness, friendliness, the warmth of his block. By the final movement the reader has accumulated half a dozen small possessives — our kano, our German, our Joe — and has registered what every Filipino in the book has registered from page one and what Joe will register only when he registers everything else, too late.
