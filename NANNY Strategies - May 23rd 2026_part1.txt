One thing worth knowing up front: the slave hunters' single greatest weapon was tracking dogs, and the Maroons' single greatest asset was terrain they knew and the enemy didn't. Almost every great defensive beat flows from one of those two facts.
ACT ONE — The Jungle as a Weapon (Defensive)
Camouflage & concealment
The forest stands up. Maroons covered head to toe in leaves and vines, motionless, as a redcoat column files past inches away — then the "underbrush" rises behind them. [H — leaf-and-branch body camouflage is specifically attributed to the Maroons; this is your signature visual.]
The lone bait (your existing beat) — sharpen it so the audience sees the trap after it springs: the chasers think they've cornered one man, and the camera pulls back to reveal the ridgeline above them was never empty. [H — single-file ambush at chokepoints is documented.]
Mud, ash, and charcoal rubbed on skin to break the human silhouette and kill shine before a night raid. [P]
False trails — deliberately broken branches and planted boot-prints leading hunters into a box canyon or a bog, while the real path goes invisibly over bare rock. [P]
Defeating the dogs (this is where the cleverness lives)
Scotch-bonnet country. Ground hot pepper and tobacco scattered thick on the trail; the lead hound buries its nose, the pack scatters, and the handler is suddenly blind. [P — pepper to foul tracking dogs is a real countermeasure, and you're literally in pepper country.]
Walking the water. The escapees wade up a streambed and the dogs lose the scent at the bank — pairs beautifully with your rescue act later. [P]

Now we're cooking. The mechanic you're describing is the genre-savvy audience is your mark — they've seen the movie, so their certainty is the thing you weaponize. Jeselnik's whole engine is "I'm building the sentence you think you're hearing." Same here: bait the cliché clean, let them lean, pull the floor.
Two principles before the list, because they make all of these land:
Pay one off straight, early. The first ambush should be exactly the ambush they expect. That's you teaching the audience your "cadence" so the later breaks actually whiplash. Jeselnik can't subvert a rhythm he hasn't established.
The loud thing is rarely the real thing — but flip which is which. Audiences (and the enemy) assume the fire is the distraction and the quiet move is the goal. Half your best reveals come from inverting that assumption.
And first — your dunder gag is genuinely excellent, so let me extend it rather than replace it, because the long con is even better than the bit: the drunk dogs don't just scatter. They follow the Maroons home. Now the hunters have lost the animals and every future pursuit is poisoned — the men no longer trust their own pack, because the pack has, once, chosen the other side. The joke becomes a permanent crack in the enemy's confidence. That's the difference between a gag and a weapon that keeps paying.

The bait that isn't bait. You set up your existing lone-man-lures-them-to-the-ambush beat — and run it straight, early, like I said. Later, same setup: lone man, the hunters grin, here we go again, find the ambush. They sweep the ridgelines, the treeline, the canyon — paranoid, thorough, three hours of it. There's no ambush. There was never an ambush. He's just a man, and while two hundred soldiers spent the afternoon hunting phantoms in the rocks, the actual raid finished forty miles away. The reveal isn't gotcha, here come the spears. It's the silence. They got played by their own memory of the last time.
The bridge that works. Trope primed: they'll sabotage the bridge they just crossed. So — they cross, the cavalry follows, the bridge holds perfectly. Audience exhales, mild disappointment, guess that wasn't it. It holds the second time too. By the third crossing the column doesn't even slow down, fully trusts it — and that's the load it was cut to fail under. The weapon wasn't the bridge. It was the enemy's confidence, which the Maroons spent two days patiently building.
The river they're supposed to lose you in. Everybody's seen the walk-up-the-streambed-to-kill-the-scent move; it was old when Tarzan did it. So the Maroons leave a blatant trail straight into the water. The hunters arrive, smug: "Classic. They went up the river. Split up, work both banks." And splitting up — predictably, exactly as their training demands — is the entire point. The trope is so well-known that the enemy's knowledge of it becomes the lever. You're not fooling people who don't know the trick. You're fooling people who do.
The bullet-catcher. The legend is famous and the audience knows it's a myth — so play to that certainty. Volley fires. Nanny stands, untouched, on the ridge. Audience braces for either cheap mysticism or a cynical debunk. Instead: she turns to the boy beside her and, flat, unbothered, tells him a freshly-fired musket takes a slow twenty count to reload and at this range black powder couldn't hit a church door — so the only dangerous second already passed, and you may as well let them watch you be unkillable. She's not catching bullets. She's a ballistics clock with a flair for theater. The myth is something she manufactures on purpose, and we just watched the factory.
The empty town is a gift. Warm cook-fires, abandoned settlement — a beat so worn it's nearly parody now. So lean all the way in: let the army win. They take the town, settle, relieved, finally a roof. No ambush comes. The trap is the town itself — it's the only flat ground for miles that floods, or its single well is the only water and it's been quietly fouled with a purgative (PG-13, nobody dies, everybody's wretched), or it simply can't be defended and the Maroons gave it away precisely because holding it costs the enemy a garrison they can't spare. The cliché says the empty town explodes. Yours says the empty town was never worth having, and now it's your problem.
The decoy and the goal, swapped. Your far-field-fire-to-cover-the-rescue setup — run it once straight so the overseers (and audience) learn it. Later, fire goes up on the far field again. The overseer, proud of himself: "It's the old trick. They want us to chase the smoke. Hold the line at the quarters, the rescue's coming there." So they all stare at the empty quarters... while the fire — the real objective this time — eats an entire season's crop unbothered. They were so sure the loud thing was the lie that they personally guaranteed it succeeded.
The pass nobody can read. Forged-papers scene primed. But there's no forgery — a Maroon walks an escapee right past the patrol holding a confident, official-looking document that is, in fact, a provisioning list or a page of scripture. The bet is simple and brutal: the patrol can't read either, but neither will admit it in front of the other. The weapon is the enemy's vanity. (And if you want the knife-twist: the "paper" is the bill of sale for the very person being walked to freedom.)
The curse that's just botany. Obeah-dread is doing real strategic work, but don't resolve it as magic vs. fooled fools. The swerve: Nanny knows precisely why men who drink from that spring or sleep in that grove fall sick — she understands the plant, the water, the season. It isn't a curse; it's that she read the land and the enemy can't. Let her own people half-believe the supernatural version and let her allow it, because she'd rather have the morale than the credit. The audience expecting a debunking gets something colder: she's not pretending to know magic. She's hiding that it's chemistry.
The infiltrator who never hid. Camouflage scene primed — the army beats the bush for the leaf-covered men. Pull back: there are no men in the bush. There's a man in a redcoat in the middle of the column, has been marching with them since dawn, counting muskets and powder. The expected reveal is the forest was alive. The real one is we were never in the forest. We were standing next to you.

A craft note on sequencing them: alternate the kind of swerve so the audience can't pre-solve your pattern either. If every flip is "the quiet thing was real," they'll catch the rhythm by the third act and you'll have built a new cliché. Mix the time-based ones (the bait), the patience ones (the bridge), the vanity ones (the pass), and the cold-competence ones (the bullets) so the only thing they can predict is that they can't predict it. That's the difference between a film that's clever and a film that feels clever, which is the one that survives a second watch.
