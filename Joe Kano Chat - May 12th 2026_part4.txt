The setup: the mother speaks little English, mostly polite functional English. She is not able to perform sweetness for the corner vendor and the doctor in the way I had been assuming because her English is not sufficient for the performance. The funeral mechanism shifts. The neighbours who mourn her sincerely are not mourning the full version of her that Joe also knew; they are mourning the limited-English version she was capable of producing for them. The version was not a performance, exactly. It was the reduced output of a person who could not access her own complexity in the language available to her external life. They are mourning what they had access to, which was a thin version of her by linguistic constraint as much as by her choice. Joe is therefore not the only person who knew the real her — he is the only person who had access to the language she was real in.
This is more nuanced than the earlier formulation and possibly more devastating. The neighbours' grief is honest and incomplete. They mourned a person they only ever partly met. Joe alone met the rest, and met it in the form of a German that is doing things English cannot do. The mother's cruelty is therefore language-specific. She was kind in English and cruel in German, not because the language determined her affect but because German was the medium of her actual self and English was the medium of her social presentation. The languages were different masks with different ranges of expressive access. She did not have the range to be cruel in English even if she had wanted to. The language did not give her the tools.
This makes the mother more sympathetic in a small way and more inscrutable in a larger way. Sympathetic because she is not strategically performing sweetness; she is constrained into something that looks like sweetness by her linguistic limits. Inscrutable because the question of who she actually is becomes less answerable — German is her real medium, but is the German cruelty her real self, or is the cruelty itself a register only available in a language that has the apparatus for it? The book does not have to settle this. The book renders her in both languages and the reader holds the question.

The question of whether to translate.
This is the architectural question and the answer matters.
Your instinct toward not-translating is correct, but the calibration matters. Three options, with different implications.
One, full no-translation. German appears on the page as German with no glosses, no italics, no English alternatives. The reader reads the German if they can; if they cannot, they receive the scene through Joe's reactions and the surrounding behaviour. This is the purest version. The cost is that significant portions of the book are inaccessible to readers without German. The benefit is that the language barrier itself becomes one of the book's structural facts — readers without German experience the scenes the way the maid does, knowing something is happening, not knowing what, reading it through bodies and tones. The reader's perceptual position is partly aligned with the maid's, which is its own kind of structural achievement.
Two, German appears with no translation but the surrounding prose carries enough information that an English-only reader can fully reconstruct the meaning from context. The German is real but is functionally redundant with the rendering around it. This is the safer version and probably the more readable. The cost: the German loses some of its specific power because the reader is not actually receiving the German, the reader is receiving the surrounding rendering. The benefit: the book remains accessible to a wider readership without sacrificing the German's textural presence.
Three, German appears occasionally and is glossed (in italics, in footnotes, in Joe's interior translation). This is the standard literary-fiction approach and is the version you have correctly indicated you want to avoid. The italics are the camel. The italics announce I am rendering a foreign language. The italics break the prose. Out.
Between option one and option two, the question is what kind of book you are writing and who you are writing it for.
Option one is the more committed, more morally serious version. It treats the German as having its own structural role rather than as a flavour. The reader who does not have German experiences the book as having a register they cannot access, and the inaccessibility is part of what the book is rendering — the mother has a register her surrounding world cannot access, and the reader is in the surrounding world. The structural alignment is precise.
Option two is more accessible and probably more practical for a reader Joseph's age. It uses German as colour rather than as structural fact. It loses the alignment between reader-position and external-world-position but gains comprehension across a larger readership.
My read: option one is the right one for this book. Several reasons.
The book has been making committed choices throughout — the no-ping ending, the choose-nothing principle, the substrate-not-content discipline, the effects-only rule. Each of these has narrowed the readership in exchange for structural integrity. Option one is consistent with this pattern. The book is not chasing the broadest possible reader. The book is doing what the book does, for the reader who can read it.
The German is also a finite presence. It will appear in maybe four or five scenes — the mother in her apartment with Joe alone, possibly with the maid present, possibly during her illness. The bulk of the book is in English. The German scenes are a specific apparatus for a specific purpose. They are not the book's primary mode. So the cost of option one is bounded — readers without German lose access to specific moments, not to the book as a whole.
The book is being written for the writer first, with the invisible-reader as the calibration tool. The invisible reader you have been calibrating against is, I suspect, a reader who can either read German or who can accept the principled withholding. The reader who cannot read German and who cannot accept the withholding is not the reader the book is for.
